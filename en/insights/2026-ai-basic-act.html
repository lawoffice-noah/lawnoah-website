South Korea’s 2026 AI Basic Act — Generative AI Labelling, High-Impact AI Duties and Business Compliance

With the implementation of South Korea’s Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Establishment of a Foundation for Trust (“AI Basic Act”), businesses developing AI or providing AI-based products and services must identify their obligations according to their role and the service’s characteristics.

Key takeaways

  • Using AI does not automatically make everyone an AI business. Distinguish developers and providers, users receiving services, and people significantly affected by the results.[2]
  • Advance notice of AI-based operation and labelling of generative output are separate obligations. Synthetic output difficult to distinguish from reality is subject to distinct disclosure and labelling standards.[3]
  • Advance high-impact assessment and safety and reliability measures are mandatory. Distinguish them from businesses’ efforts duties concerning impact assessment, verification and certification, and public bodies’ priority-consideration duties.[4][5][7]
  • The guidance period does not postpone commencement or exempt all liability. Examine the connections between a breach, investigation, corrective order and administrative fine separately.[10][11]

Disclosure and labelling for generative AI, advance assessment and safety and reliability measures for high-impact AI, and safety obligations for certain large-scale AI systems have different triggering conditions. This report distinguishes the principal provisions, the amendments effective in July 2026, and the preparations businesses should consider.[1][2]

01. Distinguish Commencement of the Act and Decree from Later Amendments

The principal provisions of the AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026. The original Enforcement Decree, Presidential Decree No. 36053, also generally took effect that day. An exception applies to the digital-medical-device part of the Act’s high-impact AI definition and the corresponding part of Schedule 1 to the Decree, which took effect on 24 January 2026.[1]

Certain provisions of Amendment Act No. 21311, promulgated on 20 January 2026, including support for groups vulnerable in relation to AI, took effect on 21 July 2026. They include a requirement to reflect the characteristics of those groups in high-impact AI impact assessments, according to the characteristics of the product or service.[1][7]

Certain digital-medical-device provisions24 January 2026Exceptions in the supplementary provisions to the Act and original Decree
Certain amendments, including support for vulnerable groups21 July 2026Additional impact-assessment provisions, among other matters
Latest consequential amendment to the current Decree20 August 2026Distinction between the current text and the original enactment

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The current Decree incorporates a consequential amendment made by Presidential Decree No. 36580. This does not mean that transparency obligations or high-impact AI duties first took effect on 20 August. Distinguish the commencement date displayed at the top of a legislative text from the date an individual obligation first applied.[1]

Acts, enforcement decrees, notices and guidelines do not all have the same legal effect

Read statutory obligations, their elaboration in the Decree, notices issued under legislative delegation, and guidance assisting interpretation and implementation separately. In particular, assess notices and guidance by their legal basis and content rather than their name alone. An example in a guideline does not itself create a licensing or certification requirement absent from the legislation.[4]

02. Identify the Business, the User and the Affected Person

Article 2, item 7 divides AI businesses into developers and AI-using businesses. Its scope includes State institutions and other covered public bodies, as well as corporations, organisations and individuals. Public-sector status therefore does not automatically exclude the provisions applicable to businesses.[2]

RoleStarting point for the legal assessment
AI developerDoes it develop and provide AI?
AI-using businessDoes it use AI supplied to it to provide an AI product or service?
UserDoes it receive an AI product or service?
Affected personDoes the product or service significantly affect the person’s life, physical safety or fundamental rights?

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Users and affected persons are separately defined in Article 2, items 8 and 9. The direct recipient of a service and the person affected by its results may be different.[2]

For example, a company receiving an AI recruitment-assessment service is not in the same position as the candidate being assessed. Even where the candidate has not personally subscribed to the service, the effect on the candidate’s fundamental rights may require consideration of whether the candidate is an “affected person”. This illustrates the distinction between the concepts; it does not determine the high-impact status of any particular system.

Internal AI Use Is Different from Providing an AI Service

Using external AI to draft internal documents should be distinguished from providing customers with a proprietary chatbot incorporating an external model. What function is provided, and to whom, matters more than the name of the tool. Neither employees’ use of AI nor reliance on an external model should, by itself, be treated as resolving the company’s statutory role.[2]

Appointment of a Domestic Representative Has a Separate Prerequisite

Article 36(1) starts with an AI business that has no address or place of business in South Korea. Within that group, appointment of a domestic representative must be considered where any of the following criteria in Article 29(1) of the Decree is met.[9]

Criterion in the DecreePrincipal content
Total revenueAt least KRW 1 trillion in the preceding year
AI-service revenueAt least KRW 10 billion in that segment in the preceding year
Domestic usersA daily average of at least one million domestic users over the three months ending at the close of the preceding year
A specified previous administrative fineA fine previously imposed under Article 43(1), item 3 of the Act

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The final criterion concerns an administrative fine for failure to comply with a cessation or corrective order. It does not cover every administrative fine, and it does not remove the statutory prerequisite of having no domestic address or place of business. A business within scope must designate the domestic representative in writing and report the designation to the Minister of Science and ICT.[9][10]

03. Check the Three Transparency Obligations Separately

Article 31 distinguishes the following three obligations.[3]

ObligationProvisionPrincipal content
Advance notice of AI-based operationArticle 31(1)Inform users before use that the product or service operates on the basis of high-impact or generative AI
Labelling of generative AI outputArticle 31(2)Indicate that the output was generated by generative AI
Disclosure or labelling of synthetic output difficult to distinguish from realityArticle 31(3)Make it clearly recognisable that synthetic audio, images, video or similar output was generated by AI

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

One advance-notice statement should not be assumed to satisfy every output-labelling obligation. Equally, the presence of a label on an output does not remove the need to examine whether notice was given before use.[3]

Adapt Advance Notice to How the Service Is Provided

Article 23(1) of the Decree identifies notice on the product, in a contract, manual or terms of use; display on the user’s screen or terminal; posting at the place of provision; and other methods recognised by the Minister in light of the product’s characteristics.[3]

In practice, check not only whether terms contain a sentence, but whether users actually receive the notice before use as they register and activate the function.

Machine-Readable Labelling Requires an Additional Notice

Article 23(2) of the Decree permits human-perceptible or machine-readable labelling. If the machine-readable method is chosen, however, a notice that the output was generated by generative AI must be provided at least once through text, audio or another form. An invisible marker alone, with no accompanying notice, should not be described as sufficient.[3]

Consider User Characteristics for Output Difficult to Distinguish from Reality

Article 23(3) of the Decree requires a method that users can readily check visually, aurally or through software or similar means, taking account of the principal users’ age and physical and social circumstances.[3]

The latter part of Article 31(3), concerning artistic or creative expression, is not a complete exemption from labelling. It permits disclosure or labelling in a way that does not interfere with the exhibition or enjoyment of the work.[3]

Assess Exceptions Within the Scope of the Relevant Obligation

Article 23(4) of the Decree allows all or part of the transparency obligations not to apply where the use of AI is obvious, where it is used solely for the business’s internal work, or in cases specified by the Minister in a notice. It is not a blanket exemption from the whole AI Basic Act or from obligations under other legislation.[3]

04. Assess Both the Field of Use and the Risks of High-Impact AI

High-impact AI and generative AI are different classifications. Article 2, item 4 concerns AI systems that may significantly affect or create risks to life, physical safety or fundamental rights and are used in a field listed in the sub-items of that provision.[2]

Healthcare and medical devices, recruitment and lending assessments, major transport systems, public-service eligibility and decisions, and student assessment in school education are only some examples. These examples do not replace the complete list. Classification requires direct comparison with each sub-item of Article 2, item 4 and the specific boundaries of the relevant field.[2][4]

For example, refining the wording of a job advertisement should be distinguished from evaluating candidates’ selection rankings. Describing both as “AI used in recruitment” is not a sufficient basis for applying the same conclusion to them.

Advance Assessment Is Mandatory; Requesting Government Confirmation Is an Available Procedure

Article 33(1) requires an advance assessment of high-impact status and permits a request for confirmation from the Minister where necessary. It does not establish a system in which every AI service needs a government licence or certification before launch.[4]

Article 25(2) of the Decree requires consideration of the field of use; the effects, seriousness and frequency of risks and sector-specific circumstances; the business’s advance assessment; and, where obtained, the specialist committee’s advice.[4]

Confirmation and Reconfirmation Have Specified Time Limits

ProcedurePeriod under the Decree

Extension of the response periodOnce, for up to 30 days, taking account of complexity, importance and related considerations
Notice of an extensionWritten notice of the reasons and extended period
Request for reconfirmationWithin 10 days of receiving the response
Response to a reconfirmation requestWithin 30 days of receipt of that request

These periods appear in Article 25(3)–(5) of the Decree. General consultation should not be confused with the statutory confirmation and reconfirmation procedures.[4]

The guidelines on criteria and examples under Article 33(3) can help businesses understand the classification. However, finding a similar example in a guideline is not the same as completing the legal assessment of one’s own service. Review the actual purpose, functions and manner of use.[4]

05. Separate Public Disclosure from Internal Records for High-Impact AI Duties

Article 34(1) requires businesses providing high-impact AI or products or services using it to implement measures covering the following matters.[5]

AreaMeasures
Risk managementEstablish and operate a risk-management plan
ExplanationDevelop and implement explanations of results, principal decision criteria and an overview of training data, to the extent technically feasible
User protectionEstablish and operate protective measures
Human involvementHuman management and supervision of high-impact AI
DocumentationPrepare and retain documents demonstrating safety and reliability measures
Other mattersSafety and reliability matters deliberated and resolved by the National AI Strategy Committee

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The existence of the sixth category is not a basis for inventing unverified additional duties. The existence and substance of any such matters require separate confirmation.

The Trade-Secret Exception Concerns Publication

Article 27(1) of the Decree requires publication at an office, place of business or website of the main elements of risk-management and explanation plans, user-protection measures, and the supervisor’s name and contact details. Information qualifying as a trade secret under the legislation may be excluded from publication.[6]

Article 27(2), however, requires documentary evidence of implementation to be retained for five years. Trade-secret status does not eliminate the requirement to create and retain the necessary implementation evidence. Set the public-disclosure scope separately from the scope of internal records.[6]

Distinguish Two Forms of Deemed Compliance

First, Article 27(3) of the Decree concerns reliance on an upstream business’s measures. Where AI is supplied by a business that has implemented the relevant measures and there is no material functional change, such as a substantial change to the original purpose or use, the recipient is deemed to have implemented the corresponding measures under Article 34(1), items 1–3. This is not a complete exemption extending to human management, supervision and documentation.[6]

Second, Article 34(3) of the Act and Article 27(5) of the Decree provide a separate mechanism recognising measures implemented under other legislation. The specific cases must be checked against Schedule 1 to the Decree, “Criteria and Procedures for Recognition of Implemented Measures”. A general statement that another law has been complied with does not establish compliance with every duty.[5][6]

A useful practical approach is to map each measure for which recognition is sought to its implementation evidence, and separately manage the measures not covered by that recognition.

Article 27(4) of the Decree also addresses an AI-using business’s requests for information and the developer’s duty to make efforts to cooperate. Specify the information needed and the arrangements for updating and delivering it at the contracting stage.[6]

06. Duties to Make Efforts Differ from Public Bodies’ Duty to Give Priority Consideration

The strength of the obligations under the AI Basic Act depends on the responsible party and the statutory wording.[4][5][7]

MeasureResponsible partyNature of the provision
Advance assessment of high-impact statusRelevant AI businessDuty to assess
Safety and reliability measuresBusiness providing high-impact AI or related products or servicesDuty to implement
Verification and certification of high-impact AIRelevant AI businessDuty to make efforts to obtain advance verification and certification
Fundamental-rights impact assessmentRelevant AI businessDuty to make efforts to conduct an advance assessment
Priority consideration of products or services based on verified or certified AIState institutions and other covered public bodies intending to use high-impact AIDuty to give priority consideration
Priority consideration of products or services that have undergone impact assessmentState institutions and other covered public bodies intending to use high-impact AI products or servicesDuty to give priority consideration

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The business’s duty to make efforts regarding verification and certification is in Article 30(3), while the public bodies’ duty to give priority consideration is in Article 30(4). Similarly, distinguish the business’s efforts duty in Article 35(1) from the public bodies’ priority-consideration duty in Article 35(2).[7]

It is therefore inaccurate either to describe a universal pre-launch certification requirement for businesses or to treat public bodies’ priority consideration as entirely optional. Nevertheless, priority consideration does not guarantee a contract or tender award to a particular business.[7]

The July Amendment Adds Consideration of Vulnerable Groups’ Characteristics

The latter part of Article 35(1), effective from 21 July 2026, requires the characteristics of groups vulnerable in relation to AI to be reflected in the assessment according to the characteristics of the product or service. Related wording was also incorporated into Article 28(1), item 1 of the Decree. Read this alongside the underlying efforts-based structure of the business’s impact-assessment obligation.[1][7]

In practice, consider people affected by the results, not only direct users. Assessment questions can address who would be disadvantaged by an error and who might find it difficult to understand an explanation or raise an objection.

07. The 10²⁶-Operation Threshold for Large-Scale AI Is Stated in the Decree

The safety obligations in Article 32 are separate from the high-impact AI duties. Article 24(1) of the Decree covers AI systems meeting all three of the following criteria.[8]

CriterionContent
Cumulative training computationAt least 10²⁶ cumulative floating-point operations used in training
Technological levelConfigured and operated using state-of-the-art AI technology at the current level of technological development
RiskPotential for widespread and serious effects on life, physical safety and fundamental rights

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The figure 10²⁶ appears directly in the Decree; it is not a threshold found only in a ministerial notice. Article 24(2) delegates the specific calculation method for cumulative training computation to a notice. Distinguish the numerical threshold from the delegation of the calculation method.[8]

Businesses within scope must identify, assess and mitigate risks throughout the lifecycle and establish a risk-management system for monitoring and responding to safety incidents. Submission of implementation results under Article 32(2) must also be considered, with the detailed delegated standards checked for the methods of implementation and submission.[8]

The 10²⁶-operation threshold does not govern the whole AI Basic Act

Falling below this threshold does not exclude generative AI disclosure and labelling obligations or high-impact AI duties. Conversely, high-impact classification does not automatically trigger the safety obligations in Article 32.[3][5][8]

08. Distinguish Administrative Fines, Investigations and the Guidance Period

Article 43(1) provides for an administrative fine of up to KRW 30 million in the following three cases.[10]

ConductRelated provision
Failure to give advance notice of high-impact or generative AI-based operationArticle 31(1)
Failure by a business within scope to designate a domestic representativeArticle 36(1)
Failure to comply with a cessation or corrective orderArticle 40(3)

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The advance-notice violation includes failure to notify users of generative AI-based services. Under Article 43(2), the fine is imposed and collected by the Minister of Science and ICT. KRW 30 million is the statutory maximum, not a uniform amount imposed for every violation.[3][10]

Output-Labelling Violations Follow a Separate Enforcement Route

Article 31(2) and (3) are not directly listed in Article 43(1). It is therefore inaccurate to say that “omitting an AI output label itself automatically results in a KRW 30 million fine”. The labelling duties nevertheless exist, together with an enforcement route through investigation and corrective orders.[10]

Article 40(1) addresses violations of the following provisions.

Provision subject to investigationPrincipal content
Article 31(2) and (3)Generative-output labelling and disclosure or labelling of synthetic output difficult to distinguish from reality
Article 32(1) and (2)Safety measures and submission of implementation results for certain large-scale AI
Article 34(1)High-impact AI businesses’ safety and reliability measures

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Documents may be requested or an investigation conducted where a violation is discovered, a suspected violation becomes known, or a report or complaint concerning such a violation is received. Failure to comply with a cessation or corrective order following the investigation may lead to a fine under Article 43(1), item 3.[10]

Distinguish Changeable Enforcement Policy from Statutory Obligations

In its implementation announcement of 21 January 2026, the Ministry of Science and ICT announced a guidance period of at least one year. It stated that investigations were expected only in highly exceptional cases involving serious social concerns, such as accidents causing death or injury or infringements of human rights.[11]

The AI Basic Act Support Desk’s legislative guidance dated 20 August 2026 also describes supporting the framework’s establishment during the guidance period. Such administrative operating policies may change, so check the latest announcements and individual notices at the time a response is required.[11]

The guidance period should not be treated as a change to the Act’s commencement date or an exemption from all liability. Nor does “at least one year” establish a fixed end date by itself. Obligations under the AI Basic Act and liabilities under other legislation, including personal-information protection law, require separate assessment.[1][11]

09. LAW NOAH Analysis — Review Functions and Roles, Not Only the Company as a Whole

The following are practical suggestions for organising scope and compliance reviews. They are not a statutory submission form or additional duties applying identically to every business.

1) Map the Function and Affected People Before Compiling a List of AI Tools

A list of the AI tools a company uses does not complete the assessment. The same model can support internal drafting, automated customer responses or candidate evaluation.

For each function, prepare a schedule connecting:

input information → recipient of the result → person affected by the result → purpose for which the result is used → human authority to review and modify it

Do not overlook people affected by the outcome by recording only contracting counterparties or service subscribers as users. Then connect the statutory role, generative functionality, possible high-impact status and required disclosure, labelling and management measures.

2) Specify the Information Needed in Supplier Contracts

A clause saying only that “the supplier complies with the AI Basic Act” may leave uncertainty about when and how the customer will receive information needed in practice.

Consider the model’s intended purpose and limitations, notification of significant functional changes, explanation and risk-management materials, labelling functions, and incident contacts and cooperation. When relying on an upstream business’s measures, distinguish the measures relied on, the changes made by the recipient and the evidence obtained.

Recognition of measures under other legislation should likewise be assessed by connecting each measure to its supporting basis, rather than relying on a general description of the supplier as “already certified”.

3) Check Public Wording, Actual Operation and Internal Records Together

Approved notice wording may be missing from the live interface, or labelling may differ at the point at which an output is delivered. Consider reviewing the registration, activation and output-delivery flow and recording the versions in use.

For high-impact AI, check whether the supervisor is appointed in name only or can actually identify errors, modify results or suspend use.

Information that is not published is not necessarily information that need not be retained. Adjust public disclosure for trade secrets while protecting necessary internal evidence through access controls.

Compliance with the AI Basic Act is not completed by attaching the same “AI used” statement to every service. Conversely, using AI does not subject every business to identical regulation.

The starting point is to identify the business’s role, the service’s functions and the people affected, then connect the applicable duties to real disclosure, labelling, management and record-keeping processes.
