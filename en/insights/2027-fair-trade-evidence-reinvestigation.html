South Korea’s 2027 Fair Trade Act Reform — Stronger FTC Evidence Disclosure and 30-Day Reinvestigation Requests

The reform expands evidence access in Fair Trade Act damages litigation and gives complainants 30 days to request reinvestigation after an FTC no-action decision.

Key points

  • The amendment takes effect on 9 September 2027.
  • Article 111 production expands across Article 109 damages actions and expressly reaches proof of the underlying violation.
  • After reasonable evidence-gathering efforts fail, a court may require production of FTC-held materials.
  • Necessary trade secrets may be produced with inspection limits and confidentiality orders.
  • A complainant may request reinvestigation within 30 days after receiving a no-action notice.
  • If justified, the case must be reinvestigated by an official different from the original investigator.

01. The reform takes effect on 9 September 2027

Act No. 21904 was promulgated on 8 September 2026 and takes effect on 9 September 2027.[1]

Its two central themes are stronger evidence access in Fair Trade Act damages litigation and a statutory reinvestigation-request procedure for complainants after an FTC no-action decision.

02. Why evidence access was strengthened

In competition cases, key evidence often sits inside the alleged violator’s pricing files, internal decision materials, emails, transaction data or the FTC’s investigative record.

The pre-amendment Act already allowed courts to request FTC records, but the amendment makes the production obligation, exceptions and trade-secret protections substantially more concrete.

03. The underlying damages cause of action is not new

Article 109 already provides a Fair Trade Act damages cause of action. The reform does not create that liability; it strengthens the special evidence mechanisms available in Article 109 damages litigation.

04. Parties must first make reasonable efforts to obtain evidence

Amended Article 110 expressly requires a party to show that, despite reasonable efforts such as seeking an Article 111 production order, it could not obtain materials needed to prove the violation, loss or amount of damages.[1]

05. Reasonable efforts become a litigation strategy

Parties should create a record of the materials they identified, the production requests they made and why the evidence could not otherwise be obtained before asking the court to obtain FTC-held materials.

06. The FTC generally produces materials after its disposition

Amended Article 110 provides that the FTC generally produces responsive materials after it has made its disposition in the underlying matter, preserving a boundary between ongoing enforcement and private litigation.

07. Four categories may be withheld

The amendment allows withholding of: leniency-related materials; FTC-created investigation or deliberation materials other than materials identified in the final decision; materials made non-public by other statutes; and trade secrets, subject to the important necessity exception.

08. Leniency materials remain specially protected

Cartel leniency depends on incentives to provide internal evidence. The amendment expressly protects Article 44(4) leniency-related materials in the FTC-production mechanism, while Article 111 continues to protect them from party-directed production orders.

09. Internal FTC investigative materials are not fully discoverable

FTC-created investigation and deliberation materials may be withheld, although materials identified in the final decision are carved out of that protection.

10. Trade secrets may still have to be produced when indispensable

If a trade secret is indispensable to proving the violation or loss, or to calculating damages, the court may require production. The court must then designate the scope of material and persons permitted to inspect it.

11. Courts may inspect withheld materials in camera

Where the FTC invokes a statutory withholding ground, the court may order presentation of the material so it can assess the claim without allowing others to see it.

12. FTC production becomes an express exception to confidentiality duties

Amended Article 119 expressly excludes Article 110 production from the FTC confidentiality prohibition, creating a clear statutory channel for court-ordered disclosure.

13. Party-directed production orders expand substantially

Current Article 111 is tied to specified violation categories. The amendment instead uses damages litigation under Article 109 as the gateway, materially broadening the mechanism.

14. Evidence of the violation itself is added

The production purpose expands from proof of loss and damages quantification to expressly include proof of the underlying violation.

15. The mechanism reaches a broader range of Fair Trade Act violations

Because the gateway becomes Article 109 damages litigation rather than a closed list of violations, the mechanism can reach other Fair Trade Act violations, including matters such as abuse of market dominance where the substantive elements are otherwise met.

16. A party may still refuse production for justifiable reasons

A holder may refuse production for a legally justifiable reason. The court may inspect the material itself to determine whether that reason is valid.

17. Necessary trade secrets may still be ordered produced

Trade-secret status alone is not a justifiable refusal where the material is indispensable to proving the violation or loss or calculating damages. Inspection must be restricted by scope and person.

18. Noncompliance can create powerful evidentiary consequences

Without justifiable reason, refusal to comply can allow the court to treat the opposing party’s assertions about the contents as true and, in specified circumstances, even treat the fact sought to be proved as true.

19. This is not US-style open-ended discovery

The mechanism remains tied to Article 109 litigation, relevance to the violation or loss, court supervision and—when FTC records are sought—a prior showing of reasonable evidence-gathering efforts.

20. Confidentiality orders already exist

Article 112 already contains a confidentiality-order regime. The 2027 reform expands it to protect trade secrets that the FTC itself produces under the strengthened Article 110 process.

21. The FTC may also seek a confidentiality order

Amended Article 112 permits the FTC, where it has produced qualifying trade-secret material, to apply for a confidentiality order protecting that information.

22. Who can be bound by a confidentiality order

The order may bind another party, a corporate representative, counsel or another person who learns the trade secret through the litigation. The information may not be used outside the litigation or disclosed beyond the permitted circle.

23. A confidentiality order requires specific substantiation

The applicant must identify the protected information, explain the risk of business harm from outside use or disclosure and specify the persons to be bound.

24. The order takes effect upon service

A confidentiality order becomes effective when the decision is served on the person to be bound. Denial or dismissal may be challenged immediately, and an order may later be cancelled if its conditions cease to exist.

25. Breach of a confidentiality order was already criminally punishable

Existing Article 127 already punishes unjustified breach with up to two years’ imprisonment or a KRW 20 million fine. Prosecution requires a complaint by the applicant for the confidentiality order.

26. Written notice of no-action decisions already existed

Article 80(3) already required written notice of the grounds, substance and reasons when the FTC took or declined action. The new Article 96-2 matters because it links no-action notice to a statutory 30-day reinvestigation request for the complainant.

27. Complainants receive a 30-day reinvestigation request right

A complainant may request reinvestigation within 30 days after receiving notice of the no-action reason. The detailed subject matter and procedure are delegated to Presidential Decree.

28. A separate review committee assesses the request

A new complainant reinvestigation-request review committee, composed of public-official and non-public-official members, evaluates whether the request is justified.

29. A different investigator must handle an approved reinvestigation

If the committee finds the request justified, the FTC must assign the reinvestigation to an investigator different from the official who initially investigated or handled the complaint.

30. Existing case law limited challenges to no-action outcomes

Under the earlier framework, Supreme Court case 98Du5682 treated a complaint as a trigger for ex officio enforcement rather than a right to demand a particular enforcement outcome, and held the no-violation/dismissal response was not an appealable administrative disposition for the complainant.[3]

Article 96-2 now creates a statutory reinvestigation route. How that new right affects older case law on appealability remains a question for future decisions.

31. A request does not automatically reopen the case

Filing within 30 days does not itself overturn the no-action decision. The review committee must find the request justified before a reinvestigation duty arises.

32. Reinvestigation and civil damages litigation are separate tracks

FTC reinvestigation concerns public enforcement; damages litigation concerns private recovery. A no-action outcome does not automatically defeat civil liability, and reinvestigation does not automatically establish damages liability.

33. The supplementary provisions set distinct application dates

Article 96-2 applies to FTC no-action decisions made on or after 9 September 2027.

The amended Articles 110 through 112 and 119 apply to damages actions filed on or after commencement. Thus an earlier violation can still be litigated under the new evidence regime if a new Article 109 action is filed after 9 September 2027.[1]

34. What claimant companies should prepare

  • the specific violation theory;
  • contracts, purchase orders and settlement data;
  • changes in price, quantity and terms;
  • emails and messages;
  • pre- and post-harm financial data;
  • FTC complaint and handling materials;
  • key evidence believed to be held by the counterparty;
  • a record of attempts to obtain it;
  • why FTC materials are necessary; and
  • a damages model and underlying data.

35. Defendant companies need evidence-management and trade-secret strategies

Potential defendants should review retention and classification of pricing files, transaction terms, internal decision records, market analysis, employee communications and trade secrets.

If production becomes necessary, protective tools such as inspection limits and confidentiality orders may be more effective than unsupported refusal.

36. Complainants should plan for the post-no-action stage from the outset

Complaint submissions should clearly organise the legal theory, core facts, important evidence, investigation needs and materials believed to be held by the respondent. Thirty days may be short if the matter must be reconstructed only after a no-action notice arrives.

37. LAW NOAH Analysis — lowering the wall between FTC enforcement and private recovery

The amendment reduces the evidentiary and procedural gap between public enforcement and private damages recovery.

It builds a chain of broader party production → proof of the violation itself → FTC production after reasonable efforts → protected production of necessary trade secrets.

For complainants it also creates no-action notice → 30-day request → review committee → reinvestigation by a different investigator.

Claimants gain more realistic evidentiary tools; defendants face greater evidence-management and confidentiality obligations; and complainants gain a statutory mechanism to challenge a no-action outcome inside the FTC process.
