South Korea’s 2026 Dark-Pattern Interface Guidelines — Account Cancellation, Recurring Payments and Price Displays

The KFTC’s October 2026 dark-pattern guidelines and November draft consumer-protection standards clarify account cancellation, recurring-payment consent, total-price displays, preselection, repeated interference and enforcement exposure.

Key points

  • The guidelines consolidate existing E-Commerce Act rules and KFTC standards into 13 operational dark-pattern types; they are not a new statute.
  • Hidden renewal, drip pricing, preselection, false hierarchy, cancellation obstruction and repeated interference are directly regulated by statute.
  • Free-to-paid conversions and recurring-payment increases require careful review of the 30-day consent, notice and cancellation framework.
  • Making cancellation harder than sign-up—or allowing app sign-up but undisclosed web-only cancellation—creates concrete enforcement risk.
  • The November 2026 draft KFTC standards would add specific examples concerning cancellation-link placement, but the draft is not yet final.

01. The KFTC issued the guidelines on 6 October 2026

On 6 October 2026, the Korea Fair Trade Commission (“KFTC”) issued its Guidelines for Operating Online Interfaces to Prevent Dark Patterns.

For each type of dark pattern, the guidelines explain the concept and consumer harm, core compliance points, detailed cautions, unlawful or inappropriate examples, and answers to common business questions.

The KFTC stated that it consolidated and updated its 2023 self-regulatory guidelines and its 2025 Q&A on six regulated dark-pattern types, while reflecting monitoring and enforcement experience.

02. The guidelines did not create a new statute

The core legal duties and prohibitions already appear in South Korea’s E-Commerce Act.

The Act directly regulates consent and notice for recurring-payment price increases and free-to-paid conversions, drip pricing, preselection, false hierarchy, obstruction of cancellation or withdrawal, and repeated interference.

The 2026 guidelines are therefore best understood as an integrated operational guide for applying existing law and administrative standards to real websites and mobile applications.

03. Statutes, KFTC standards and guidance do not have identical legal force

The E-Commerce Act, its Enforcement Decree and Enforcement Rule are binding legislation.

The KFTC’s Consumer Protection Guidelines for Electronic Commerce distinguish between general standards for applying the law and recommendations intended to promote voluntary compliance.

The October 2026 guidelines do not independently create new administrative fines or suspension grounds. They remain highly relevant, however, because they show how the KFTC is likely to interpret and enforce existing rules.

04. A November draft also addresses where account-cancellation links should appear

On 6 November 2026, one month after issuing the guidelines, the KFTC published a draft amendment to its Consumer Protection Guidelines.

The draft would identify as a potential unlawful obstruction of withdrawal a design in which a sign-up button appears on the first screen but the cancellation link is hidden outside the logged-in landing page or a directly linked next page such as “My Page” or “Account Management.”

It also gives examples involving unnecessary identity verification required only at cancellation, or mandatory surveys that block cancellation.

As of the publication date of this article, this remains a draft administrative notice. The final text must be checked separately.

05. The earlier 19 types have been reorganised into 13

The 2023 self-regulatory guidelines classified dark patterns into four categories and 19 types. The 2026 guidelines reorganise them into four categories and 13 types.

Six earlier categories—including sneak-into-basket practices, click fatigue, emotional language, countdown claims, low-stock notices and social-activity notices—are no longer separate types in the 13-type framework.

06. Not all 13 types are separate newly enacted prohibitions

Six types are specifically regulated by the E-Commerce Act: hidden renewal, drip pricing, preselection, false hierarchy, obstruction of cancellation or withdrawal, and repeated interference.

The remaining types may still fall under the Act’s broader prohibition on false or deceptive solicitation, trading, obstruction of cooling-off rights or contract termination, as well as the Fair Labelling and Advertising Act, depending on the facts.

The Supreme Court has held that actual consumer conversion is unnecessary: concealing, omitting or minimising information capable of influencing a purchase may be enough where consumers could be misled.

07. Hidden renewal — free trials require a separate look at paid conversion consent

Article 13(6) of the E-Commerce Act applies where a recurring-payment price increases or a service previously supplied free of charge changes to a paid recurring arrangement.

The business must obtain advance consent concerning the timing of the change, the old and new prices and the payment method, and must notify the consumer of cancellation conditions, methods and effects.

The Enforcement Decree uses a 30-day period before the increase or conversion. Starting a free trial is not, by itself, blanket consent to every later paid charge.

08. Broad consent collected at initial sign-up may not be enough

A sentence in the original terms stating that the consumer agrees to any future paid conversion or price change does not necessarily replace the separate process required at the actual conversion or increase.

The interface should allow the consumer to understand when the price changes, the new amount and the payment method before giving consent.

Failure to obtain consent or provide the required notice under Article 13(6) may attract an administrative fine of up to KRW 10 million.

09. Drip pricing — the first price screen should generally show the mandatory total

Drip pricing occurs where the first screen displays a low price but later stages add mandatory service fees, facility charges, delivery costs or other unavoidable amounts.

As a rule, the E-Commerce Act requires the first price display to show the total amount the consumer must pay to purchase or use the product or service.

10. The exception requires both a legitimate reason and prescribed disclosure

The Act recognises an exception where there is a legitimate reason why the total cannot be displayed on the first screen and that reason is disclosed in the manner prescribed by the Enforcement Rule.

The first screen must identify excluded items and explain why the mandatory amount cannot be calculated uniformly. Where space is limited, detailed reasons may appear on a page directly linked from the first screen.

A vague statement that “charges may vary by location or option” should not automatically be treated as sufficient.

11. Preselection — paid extras should be actively selected by the consumer

Problems arise where paid add-ons, insurance, memberships or extra products are pre-checked and remain in the transaction unless the consumer opts out.

The Act prohibits presenting a choice concerning another product, service or membership with the consumer’s purchase intention already marked before the consumer makes the choice.

Convenience features such as filling in a previously used delivery address do not necessarily amount to prohibited preselection because they do not solicit a separate purchase.

12. False hierarchy — making only the acceptance button prominent can be risky

A typical example is a large, vivid “Agree and continue” button paired with a small grey “No thanks” link placed elsewhere on the screen.

The Act prohibits visually significant differences in size, shape or colour between choices concerning purchase, sign-up, contract formation, cancellation, withdrawal or termination where the design could make consumers believe that only one choice is available or mandatory.

This does not mean that every banner and information box must look identical. The direct statutory focus is the consumer’s decision interface.

13. Obstruction of cancellation or withdrawal — exit should not be harder than entry

A service may create legal risk where sign-up takes only a few taps but cancellation requires a call centre, repeated agent contact, a separate email and documentary identity proof.

The Act prohibits, without legitimate reason, making cancellation, withdrawal or termination more complicated than purchase or sign-up, or restricting the consumer to a different method from the one used to enter the transaction.

14. The status of the ‘My Page’ recommendation depends on timing

The October 2026 guidelines recommended placing cancellation access on the logged-in landing page or a directly accessible account-management page rather than hiding it in unexpected menus such as privacy editing or security settings.

The November draft Consumer Protection Guidelines go further by proposing to identify certain hidden placements as examples that may constitute unlawful obstruction.

The accurate description at present is that the October document contains an operational recommendation, while the KFTC is separately moving to incorporate the point into its formal application standards.

15. App sign-up combined with web-only cancellation has already attracted enforcement

In a digital music case, the provider did not offer mid-term cancellation inside the app, allowed it only through the website, and did not tell app users attempting to cancel about that route.

The Supreme Court left standing the finding that this was a deceptive obstruction of contract termination. It separately reversed the portion concerning the statutory prerequisites for a surcharge imposed in place of business suspension.

Businesses should therefore review cancellation channels, navigation paths, consumer notices and the relative burden across app, mobile-web and desktop-web environments.

16. Account withdrawal, contract termination and statutory cooling-off rights are different

Account deletion or termination of a continuing contract is legally distinct from the statutory cooling-off right available for certain distance transactions.

A business disputing a cooling-off right cannot end the analysis by stating “used products are non-refundable” or “custom orders cannot be cancelled.”

The Supreme Court has held that the business bears the burden of proving both the existence of a statutory restriction and compliance with the required notice concerning that restriction.

17. Repeated interference — the Act says ‘repeatedly’; KFTC standards specify two or more requests

The E-Commerce Act prohibits repeatedly asking a consumer to change an already made decision through pop-ups or similar methods in a manner that obstructs free decision-making.

The statute itself does not specify a number. The KFTC Consumer Protection Guidelines use two or more repeated change requests as a more concrete application standard.

The “two or more” criterion is therefore an administrative application standard, not a number written directly into the Act.

18. A seven-day ‘do not show again’ option may fall within the statutory exception

The Act recognises an exception where the consumer can choose not to receive the same change request for a prescribed period.

The Enforcement Decree sets that period at seven days. An interface that repeats the request should therefore consider offering a “Do not show again for seven days” option from the first request.

Passive banner exposure may still differ from intrusive pop-ups that obstruct use of the service.

19. False discounts — the evidentiary basis for the reference price matters

A claim such as “KRW 100,000 list price, today KRW 50,000, 50% off” may be problematic where the product was not genuinely sold at the reference price and no reasonable basis exists for it.

The guidelines identify inflated reference prices, false current prices and overstated discount rates as false-discount practices.

Businesses should retain the basis for the comparison price and actual sales records supporting the displayed discount.

20. False recommendations — selectively manufacturing positive reviews can create liability

Typical conduct includes deleting unfavourable reviews without a reasonable basis, making them hard to locate, or manufacturing favourable reviews.

Responsibility does not necessarily disappear because a marketing agency or review vendor performed the work.

In a sponsored-blog case, the Seoul High Court recognised advertiser responsibility where the advertiser funded the campaign and substantially participated in preparing and reviewing the content.

21. Bait selling — do not advertise a product that was never genuinely available

A low-priced product may be used to attract consumers, who are then told it is unavailable and encouraged to buy a more expensive substitute.

Advertising a product as available where there was no genuine intention to sell it may constitute false or deceptive consumer solicitation.

A legitimate sell-out differs from a product that was never intended to be supplied, so inventory and sold-out status should be updated promptly.

22. Disguised advertising — paid promotion should not look like an independent review or article

Content produced by the business or by a person with an economic relationship to it may be problematic if presented as an ordinary consumer review, objective news article, research result or spontaneous endorsement.

Payment, free products or other material connections should be disclosed in a form that consumers can easily find and understand.

A disclosure visible only after opening “More,” or buried in the middle of the content, may be inadequate.

23. Trick questions — avoid double negatives and bundled decisions

A question such as “Do you not disagree with refusing promotional messages?” can confuse consumers about what their response means.

Combining a product purchase, membership registration and marketing consent into one button similarly prevents independent decisions.

Each question should convey one clear meaning, and legally distinct consents should be separated.

24. Hidden information — material transaction terms should not be difficult to find

Hiding or minimising fees, additional charges, contract duration, automatic renewal, supply timing or cancellation conditions can move the issue beyond design and into deceptive solicitation or obstruction of termination.

The Supreme Court has held that concealing, omitting or minimising purchase-relevant facts can be deceptive even without proof that consumers were actually induced.

The web-only cancellation case illustrates the same principle where material information about the available exit route was withheld from app users.

25. Comparison obstruction — consumers should be able to compare actual prices rationally

Comparison becomes difficult when similar products use inconsistent price units, contract periods, included options or fee disclosures.

The issue is not mere visual inconsistency. The question is whether the presentation materially prevents consumers from comparing transaction terms and making an informed choice.

26. Removal from the 13-type list does not mean a practice is automatically lawful

Some practices identified in the 2023 framework—such as sneak-into-basket designs, click fatigue, emotional language, countdown timers, low-stock claims and social-activity notices—are no longer separate categories.

That does not make every use of them lawful.

False stock claims, fabricated deadlines, invented consumer activity or concealed material information may still violate broader provisions of the E-Commerce Act or advertising law.

27. Directly regulated types can lead to corrective orders and administrative fines

Violations of Article 13(6) and the five interface prohibitions in Article 21-2 can lead to orders to cease the conduct, perform statutory duties, publish the corrective order, and take consumer-prevention or redress measures.

Each can also be subject to an administrative fine of up to KRW 10 million. Broader false or deceptive solicitation under Article 21(1) may attract different or higher sanctions depending on the applicable provision.

Business suspension or a substitute surcharge may also arise in qualifying cases, while the Act includes rules limiting duplicate administrative fines where a surcharge has been imposed for the same conduct.

28. Lack of awareness or intention does not necessarily avoid administrative sanctions

The Supreme Court has confirmed in an E-Commerce Act advertising case that administrative sanctions may generally be imposed without proof of intent or negligence, absent special circumstances, and that subjective awareness of falsity is not always required.

Responsibility therefore does not automatically disappear because an interface was created by a development team or agency without management or legal review.

Whether there was a justifiable reason making the violation non-attributable remains a separate question.

29. Online businesses must review the user journey, not only the contract

Dark-pattern compliance cannot be completed by reviewing terms and conditions alone.

Legal risk may arise from which button appears first, which options are preselected, how many clicks cancellation requires, when the full price appears, how often a pop-up returns, and how acceptance and refusal are visually presented.

Compliance review is more effective when integrated into planning, design and development rather than added only after the interface is complete.

30. Immediate compliance checklist for online businesses

Recurring payments

  • Is separate consent obtained for free-to-paid conversion?
  • Are required consent and notice completed within the 30-day framework before a price increase?
  • Are non-consenting consumers protected from automatic charging?
  • Are cancellation conditions, methods and effects explained?

Price and choice screens

  • Does the first price screen include mandatory costs?
  • If a total cannot be calculated, are exclusions and reasons disclosed in the prescribed manner?
  • Are paid options left unselected until the consumer chooses them?
  • Are acceptance and refusal choices presented without misleading visual hierarchy?

Cancellation and pop-ups

  • Is cancellation materially more difficult than sign-up?
  • Can a consumer who signed up in the app cancel without being forced into an undisclosed channel?
  • Can the cancellation link be found from an intuitive account-management screen?
  • Are rejected choices repeatedly challenged?
  • If requests repeat, can the consumer suppress them for at least seven days?

Reviews and advertising

  • Are unfavourable reviews retained unless there is a legitimate moderation reason?
  • Are advertising, sponsorship and material connections clearly disclosed?
  • Are unavailable or never-intended products avoided as bait?
  • Does the business review final agency-created content and disclosures?

LAW NOAH Analysis — the interface now needs legal review as much as the contract

Legal risk in online commerce no longer sits only in the terms and conditions. Button colour, click count, pop-up sequence, price placement and the cancellation path itself can become part of the legal analysis.

The October guidelines and the November draft standards show that the KFTC is treating “easy entry, difficult exit” design as a compliance issue rather than merely a user-experience problem.

At the same time, businesses should not overstate every recommendation as an independent statutory duty. The practical task is to distinguish direct statutory prohibitions, KFTC application standards, voluntary guidance and draft rules that are not yet final, and to manage each at the appropriate level.
