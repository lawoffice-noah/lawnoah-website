South Korea’s 2026 AI Virtual-Person Advertising Rules — AI Doctors, Virtual Consumers and Influencers

A realistic AI-generated doctor, consumer or influencer used to endorse a product should be clearly identified as virtual. That disclosure does not cure fabricated testimonials, misleading expert endorsements, undisclosed sponsorship or unsupported performance claims.

Key points

  • A realistic AI-generated virtual person used for an endorsement should be clearly disclosed as virtual.
  • Disclosure does not legitimise fabricated testimonials or unsupported expert recommendations.
  • Sponsorship and other economic relationships require separate review.
  • From 27 November 2026, cosmetics face a separate statutory rule on AI virtual experts.

01. The KFTC guideline has applied since 1 June 2026

The KFTC amended its Guidelines for Review of Endorsements and Testimonials, effective 1 June 2026. The current guideline is KFTC Established Rule No. 499.

The amendment expressly addresses AI-generated virtual persons, provides disclosure examples and clarifies fabricated experiential claims.[1][2]

02. This is not a separate ‘AI advertising statute’

The guideline does not create a standalone criminal offence or administrative fine merely for departing from a sample disclosure phrase.

It is a detailed review standard for assessing unfair advertising under the Fair Labeling and Advertising Act and its Enforcement Decree.[1][3][4]

Supreme Court case law treats such administrative standards as important, but not exclusive, criteria for statutory illegality.[8]

03. The listed examples are neither exhaustive nor automatically determinative

Conduct not expressly listed may still constitute unfair advertising if it is likely to mislead consumers and impair fair trading.

Conversely, conduct resembling a listed example is not automatically unlawful where the advertisement does not actually create material consumer deception or fair-trading harm.[1]

04. Not every AI-generated image requires a virtual-person disclosure

The rule focuses on a virtual person generated through AI or similar technology who is difficult to distinguish from a real person and who gives an endorsement or testimonial.[1]

05. Mere appearance is not necessarily an endorsement

The endorsement guideline is concerned with representations consumers may understand as the independent view of a third party.

A silent virtual model is different from a virtual consumer saying, “I used this myself and recommend it.”[1][14]

06. Virtual consumers, celebrities and experts can all be covered

The KFTC expressly gives examples involving a virtual consumer, virtual celebrity and virtual expert.[1]

The rule therefore reaches beyond the familiar concept of a virtual influencer.

07. Text-based posts should disclose at the title or beginning

For blogs, online cafés and other text-centred content, disclosure at the title or beginning of the post is given as an appropriate method.

Examples include wording equivalent to “This post contains an AI-generated virtual person” or “Virtual person included.”[1]

08. Photos and videos should disclose near the person while the person appears

For photos and videos, the KFTC’s example is to display wording such as “virtual person” near the person while the virtual person appears.[1]

09. Tiny or remote disclosure may not work

The key is whether consumers can recognise the virtual nature of the person clearly and easily.[1]

Very small text, low contrast, remote placement, momentary display or disclosure hidden behind another click can undermine effectiveness.

The Supreme Court has held that deceptive advertising is generally assessed at the advertising stage; facts learned later in the transaction do not ordinarily cure the original deception.[10]

10. ‘I personally used it’ is a high-risk statement

Identifying a character as virtual does not permit fabricated experience claims.

If a virtual person purports to base an endorsement on product use or personal experience, the claimed experience must correspond to an actual experiential fact.[1]

11. A virtual-person label does not turn a fictional testimonial into truth

Disclosure answers whether the endorser is real. It does not establish that the claimed effect or experience is true.

The Supreme Court has held that consumer-testimonial advertising can be false or exaggerated where the experience is objectively inaccurate or overstated in a way likely to mislead consumers.[9]

12. Advertisers bear the burden of substantiating factual claims

Article 5 of the Fair Labeling and Advertising Act requires advertisers to be able to substantiate factual matters stated in advertising.[3]

Where a testimonial is likely to be understood as a result generally achievable by consumers, the advertiser may need to substantiate that broader proposition, not merely one exceptional example.[9]

13. Virtual-expert advertising is judged by its overall and ultimate impression

The Supreme Court assesses consumer deception by the overall and ultimate impression on an ordinary consumer exercising ordinary care.[8]

A small “virtual person” label may not neutralise an overall presentation using a white coat, hospital setting, professional title, medical terminology and authoritative delivery to suggest a genuine doctor’s recommendation.

14. The KFTC itself uses the virtual doctor as a leading example

In its November 2026 public campaign, the KFTC expressly warned that a trustworthy-looking doctor on screen may in fact be an AI-created virtual person.[13]

15. From 27 November 2026, cosmetics face a more direct statutory prohibition

From 27 November 2026, Article 13(1)4 of the Cosmetics Act directly prohibits specified advertising using realistic AI-generated audio, images or video that is likely to make consumers believe that doctors, dentists, Korean medicine doctors, veterinarians, pharmacists, professors or other relevant experts endorse, recommend, guide or use a cosmetic product.[7]

16. For cosmetics, virtual-person disclosure and expert-misrepresentation are separate issues

The KFTC guideline focuses on disclosure that the person is virtual.

The Cosmetics Act separately prohibits specified AI virtual-expert advertising where the presentation itself is likely to create the prohibited expert impression.[7]

17. Virtual-person disclosure and sponsorship disclosure communicate different facts

Disclosure that a person is virtual is different from disclosure of a material economic relationship between an advertiser and an endorser.

The KFTC guideline requires disclosure of economic interests capable of affecting the credibility of an endorsement, by the advertiser or endorser as applicable.[1]

18. ‘Virtual person’ alone does not disclose that the post is sponsored

A paid virtual influencer labelled only “virtual person” tells consumers that the character is fictional but not that the operator is being paid by an advertiser.

Where both facts matter, both should be made clear.[12]

19. Labels such as AD, Sponsor or Ambassador alone may be insufficient

The guideline expressly gives foreign-language-only expressions such as Advertisement, AD, PR, Collaboration, Partnership, Sponsor, spon, Collabo, Thanks to and Ambassador as inappropriate examples for Korean-language consumer content.[15]

20. Product performance claims by virtual persons still require substantiation

If a virtual person makes objectively verifiable claims about product efficacy or performance, ordinary advertising-substantiation rules apply.

The KFTC amended its substantiation notice effective 3 September 2026 to clarify expectations for claims involving AI and other new technologies.[5][11]

21. ‘AI recommended this’ can itself overstate objectivity

Statements that an AI selected or recommended a product can imply superior objectivity, accuracy, lack of bias or personal optimisation.

The KFTC separately circulated a draft general AI advertising guideline for comments from 22 October to 11 November 2026.[6]

As of 27 November 2026, it should not be presented as a final binding guideline.

23. AI manipulation of a real person raises different issues

The virtual-person rule primarily concerns a person who does not actually exist.

Using AI to alter the face or voice of a real celebrity, doctor or influencer to fabricate an endorsement raises additional issues concerning truthfulness, individual rights and deceptive advertising.

24. Outsourcing AI production does not automatically remove advertiser responsibility

An advertiser cannot assume responsibility disappears merely because an agency or AI production vendor created the content.

Korean enforcement materials in viral-marketing cases recognise advertiser responsibility where the advertiser planned and funded the campaign and participated in drafting, reviewing or directing the content.[12]

25. Violations can lead to more than a revised caption

If the advertisement constitutes unfair advertising under Article 3, the KFTC may order cessation, publication of the corrective order, corrective advertising or other remedial measures.[3]

Article 9 permits an administrative surcharge of up to 2% of relevant sales, or up to KRW 500 million where sales are absent or difficult to calculate under the applicable rules.

Unfair advertising can also carry criminal penalties of up to two years’ imprisonment or a fine of up to KRW 150 million.[3]

26. The KFTC has said it will monitor virtual-person disclosure

When announcing the June amendment, the KFTC said it would monitor advertisements using AI virtual persons without guideline-compliant disclosure and encourage correction.[2]

Its November public campaign used an AI doctor as a prominent example.[13]

27. Pre-publication checklist for AI virtual-person advertising

Is the person virtual?

  • Does the person actually exist?
  • Is the character AI-generated and realistic?
  • Could consumers mistake the character for a real consumer, celebrity or expert?

Is it an endorsement?

  • Is the person merely a model?
  • Does the content appear to express an independent third-party view?
  • Is it a testimonial or expert recommendation?

Disclosure

  • For text, is disclosure at the title or beginning?
  • For photo/video, is it near the person while the person appears?
  • Is the text sufficiently visible without another click?

Substance

  • Is any experience claim real?
  • Is any expert claim supported?
  • Are efficacy and performance claims substantiated?

Economic relationship

  • Did the operator receive advertising fees, sponsorship or commissions?
  • Are virtual-person disclosure and sponsorship disclosure separately addressed?

Sector-specific rules

  • Does the product fall under cosmetics, medical, health, financial or another regulated sector?
  • Is a stricter sectoral statute applicable?

LAW NOAH Analysis — separate ‘who is speaking’ from ‘what is being said’

AI advertising compliance is not solved by adding the word “AI”. Advertisers should separately ask who appears to be speaking, why the speaker is promoting the product, what factual or experiential claims are being made, and whether those claims can be proved.

A practical review sequence is concept → AI production → endorsement review → virtual-person disclosure → sponsorship disclosure → substantiation → sector-specific legal review.
