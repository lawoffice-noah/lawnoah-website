South Korea’s 2026 Origin-Labelling Reform — Online Platforms Must Notify Marketplace Sellers of Origin-Labelling Rules

From 22 October 2026, online marketplace intermediaries must notify sellers of South Korea’s origin-labelling rules before or when accepting the intermediation request.

Key points

  • The reform has been in force since 22 October 2026.
  • Online marketplace intermediaries must notify sellers of origin-labelling scope, methods, prohibited false or misleading claims and sanctions when accepting intermediation requests.
  • The reform does not make platforms responsible for independently verifying the true origin of every listed product.
  • One of three notice methods may be used: permanent posting plus a prompt to review it, a separate notice screen, or individual notice by paper/email/text.
  • The Enforcement Decree’s individual fine benchmarks are KRW 1 million for a first violation, KRW 2 million for a second and KRW 4 million thereafter, subject to the general adjustment rules.
  • Sellers remain responsible for accurate origin labelling and for avoiding false or misleading origin representations.

01. The reform has been in force since 22 October 2026

Act No. 21563 amended South Korea’s Act on Origin Labeling of Agricultural and Fishery Products on 21 April 2026, with the new platform-notice duty taking effect on 22 October 2026.[1]

The Enforcement Decree and Enforcement Rule were amended in October 2026 to specify the required content, permitted notice methods and administrative-fine benchmarks.

02. Why platforms were given a new duty

Sellers already had duties to label origin correctly and avoid false origin claims. Certain large-store operators and home-shopping broadcasters also had existing duties not to ignore known or reasonably knowable violations.

Online marketplace intermediaries, however, did not have the same preventative notice duty. The reform adds that front-end compliance step as online and delivery-platform transactions continue to expand.[5]

03. Who bears the new notice duty

Article 6(6) applies to a person engaged in mail-order brokerage as defined under South Korea’s E-Commerce Act.[2]

Open marketplaces, online shopping platforms and delivery apps may therefore be covered where their actual business model constitutes intermediation. The label the operator uses for itself is not decisive.

In a different statutory context, Supreme Court Order 2019Ma5464 likewise examined an online platform’s actual role and economic structure rather than relying only on formal labels.[8] Because that case concerned book-price regulation, it should not be treated as a categorical test for this Act.

04. Platforms combining direct sales and intermediation should separate the two

The Ministry of Agriculture’s official guidance states that a platform’s own direct-purchase or direct-sale business is not, by itself, subject to the new Article 6(6) notice duty.[6]

Where the same operator also intermediates sales by third-party sellers, the notice duty applies to the intermediation side. Clear operational separation between first-party and marketplace transactions is therefore prudent.

05. The platform duty is notice, not origin verification

The statute does not require the marketplace to investigate whether each product is actually Korean, Chinese or from another country, nor does it require the platform to write the seller’s origin field.

Article 6(6) requires the intermediary to notify the seller of the origin-labelling regime so that the seller does not engage in prohibited conduct. The Ministry’s guidance expressly characterises this as a preventative information duty rather than a substantive origin-verification duty.[6]

06. This differs from existing duties not to ignore known violations

Existing Article 6(4) and (5) provisions address specified operators that know or could know of origin-labelling violations and nevertheless fail to take required measures.

The new Article 6(6), by contrast, directly asks whether the online intermediary gave the required notice. The administrative-fine provisions also treat the earlier non-neglect duties and the new notice duty separately.

The Ministry’s official guidance states that proper compliance with Article 6(6) does not itself create a separate continuing duty under that paragraph to police every later seller violation.[6] That interpretation does not eliminate liability that could arise under other laws or from the platform’s own separate conduct.

07. When notice is required — when intermediation is requested

The statute uses the phrase “when mail-order brokerage is requested”, and the Ministry’s guidance describes the notice as being required whenever such a request is received.

Embedding notice in seller onboarding is therefore the clearest design. Whether a later renewal, new product category or restructuring constitutes a fresh intermediation request will depend on the particular arrangement; public materials do not yet provide a universal rule for every variation.

08. What must be notified

Article 5-2 of the Enforcement Decree identifies three categories:[3]

  1. the agricultural, fishery and processed products subject to origin labelling and the applicable labelling methods;
  2. prohibitions on false or misleading origin representations under Article 6(1) and (2), together with sanctions;
  3. other origin-management matters jointly designated by the agriculture and oceans ministers.

Platforms should therefore maintain the notice as the underlying rules and joint notices change.

09. One of three notice methods may be used

Article 4-2 of the Enforcement Rule permits one of three methods:[4]

  • Permanent posting on the seller system’s initial screen or notice board, together with a prompt directing the seller to review the posting;
  • a separate notice screen designed so the seller can recognise the information;
  • individual notice by paper, email, text message or another electronic document.

10. A buried notice-board post may be insufficient

The first method requires more than simply uploading a notice: it combines continuous posting with a prompt to check the notice.

The Ministry’s guidance gives examples such as a persistent seller-centre banner, a pinned notice plus onboarding prompt, or a permanent initial-screen link. A post that quickly disappears, is hidden deep in submenus or is never brought to the seller’s attention may be inadequate.[6]

11. A single clause hidden in lengthy terms can also be risky

The separate-screen method should provide a genuine opportunity to recognise the content.

The guidance gives dedicated pop-ups, standalone pages, confirmation buttons and separate checkboxes as suitable designs, and identifies a single buried clause inside lengthy general terms as a poor example.[6]

12. Individual notice by email, text or paper is permitted

A platform may send a dedicated origin-labelling notice to the seller’s registered email or phone number, or provide it as a separate paper document.

Combining it inconspicuously with unrelated marketing information is less defensible because it weakens actual notice.

13. Keep evidence that notice was actually given

In an enforcement dispute, the important question may be when, to whom and in what version the required information was delivered.

Platforms should retain notice versions, posting dates, pinning history, seller acknowledgments, checkbox logs and email or text delivery records. The Ministry’s guidance also recommends preserving evidence of notice.

14. The Ministry has provided a standard notice template

The Ministry has published a standard form covering the core subjects: what must be labelled, how origin should be displayed, what false or misleading representations are prohibited, and what sanctions can follow.

A platform may adapt presentation to its interface, but the legally required substance should not be omitted.

15. Sellers’ existing origin-labelling duties remain

Online sellers already had to display origin for products subject to the Act. The reform does not transfer that substantive responsibility to the intermediary.

The division of responsibility is straightforward: the platform gives the compliance notice; the seller labels the actual product correctly.

16. Online origin information should be visible before purchase

Origin information in online commerce should be presented where consumers can identify it before completing a purchase, such as near the product or price information or in a clearly identifiable origin field on the product page.

Product-specific requirements should be checked against the Enforcement Rule and the detailed origin-labelling notice.

17. Failure to label and false labelling are different violations

Failing to provide a required origin label and affirmatively giving a false or misleading origin representation are not the same offence.

Non-labelling and method violations generally sit within the administrative-fine framework, while false, misleading or disguised origin representations can trigger criminal liability.

18. A correct “Korean origin” field may still be misleading in context

A technically correct origin field does not necessarily cure misleading surrounding presentation.

In Supreme Court case 2013Do14586, even though the formal origin field stated domestic origin, the use of a well-known regional production name elsewhere on the packaging could create a misleading impression about the actual production area and constitute a prohibited misleading origin representation.[7]

Online sellers should therefore review the product title, hero image, banners, detailed description and regional-origin language as a whole.

19. False origin labelling may carry up to seven years’ imprisonment or a KRW 100 million fine

Article 14(1) provides for up to seven years’ imprisonment or a criminal fine of up to KRW 100 million, and permits both sanctions to be imposed together, for violations of Article 6(1) or (2).[2]

20. Repeat offences within five years face stronger penalties

If a person whose sentence under Article 14(1) has become final violates Article 6(1) or (2) again within five years, Article 14(2) provides for one to ten years’ imprisonment or a fine of KRW 5 million to KRW 150 million, with cumulative punishment also possible.[2]

21. Platforms that fail to give notice face administrative fines

Article 18(1) provides a statutory ceiling of KRW 10 million for violating the new notice duty.

The Enforcement Decree’s individual benchmarks are:[3]

Violation countBenchmark
FirstKRW 1 million
SecondKRW 2 million
ThirdKRW 4 million
Fourth or laterKRW 4 million

The general rules allow adjustment within one-half of the individual benchmark depending on the circumstances, subject to the statutory ceiling.

22. Formalistic notice can still leave enforcement risk

The Ministry’s guidance focuses on continuity and recognisability.

A hidden submenu, transient notice, posting without any prompt to review it or a clause lost inside lengthy terms may not demonstrate meaningful compliance merely because the wording existed somewhere on the platform.

23. Seller enforcement disclosures may include the intermediary’s website address

Where a seller’s origin-labelling violation is publicly disclosed under the Act, the Enforcement Decree may require disclosure of the website address of the mail-order intermediary through which the seller sold the product.[3]

Seller misconduct can therefore create reputational consequences for the marketplace even where substantive origin labelling remains the seller’s responsibility.

24. Embedding notice in seller onboarding is the simplest control

A practical workflow is: seller registration → intermediation request → origin-rules notice → acknowledgement → timestamped record → approval.

For a platform with many sellers, automated evidence is more reliable than relying on individual staff memory.

25. Giving existing sellers a one-time notice is prudent

The statute ties the duty to the time when intermediation is requested and does not expressly impose a universal retroactive re-notice duty on every seller whose onboarding was completed before commencement.

Renewals, new requests and material changes may raise fresh questions. As an operational control, giving all active sellers the same one-time notice and retaining evidence is a straightforward way to standardise compliance.

26. Platform checklist

  • Separate first-party direct sales from third-party intermediation.
  • Embed the origin-rules notice in the onboarding or intermediation-request process.
  • Include all categories required by Article 5-2 of the Enforcement Decree.
  • If using a notice-board method, ensure both permanent posting and a prompt to review it.
  • Do not rely solely on a clause buried in general terms.
  • Retain email, text and acknowledgement records.
  • Store acknowledgement time and notice version.
  • Monitor changes to joint ministerial notices.
  • Set a policy for existing active sellers.
  • Preserve evidence for possible administrative-fine proceedings.

27. Seller checklist

  • Determine whether each product is subject to origin labelling.
  • Display the actual origin accurately on the product page.
  • Make the origin visible before purchase.
  • Check which ingredients of processed foods require origin disclosure.
  • Use the correct method for mixed domestic and imported inputs.
  • Update origin information when suppliers change.
  • Ensure delivery-app menu origin information matches actual ingredients.
  • Review titles, images and marketing language for misleading regional-origin implications.
  • Update online labels promptly when origin changes.
  • Retain records supporting the stated origin.

28. LAW NOAH Analysis — platform responsibility has expanded from neutrality to compliance communication

The amendment does not turn online marketplaces into inspectors responsible for verifying the true origin of every listed product.

It does, however, add a preventative role at the point where a seller enters the marketplace. The platform can no longer treat the seller’s awareness of origin-labelling law as wholly outside its compliance process.

The allocation is relatively clear: the platform must communicate the rules; the seller must label the actual product accurately.

The most efficient response is to systematise a dedicated notice during onboarding or the intermediation request, capture acknowledgement, retain logs and version the notice. The reform is therefore best understood not as a new origin-verification duty, but as a requirement to build a provable compliance-communication workflow.
