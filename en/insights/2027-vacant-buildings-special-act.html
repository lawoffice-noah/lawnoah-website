South Korea’s 2027 Vacant Buildings Special Act — Integrated Management from One-Year Vacant Homes to Two-Year Stalled Construction

From 9 September 2027, South Korea integrates rules for vacant homes, older unused non-residential buildings and construction stalled for at least two years, with new planning, enforcement and reuse tools.

Key points

  • The Act takes effect on 9 September 2027.
  • A home can qualify after one year of non-use; qualifying non-residential buildings generally require at least 20 years since completion plus one year of non-use.
  • Construction stalled for at least two years is brought into the same framework.
  • High-risk buildings can face mandatory orders, ex officio demolition and repeated enforcement charges.
  • Promotion zones may receive relief up to 130% of statutory planning maxima, while new reuse mechanisms include urban infill facilities.
  • Delegated details must be rechecked once subordinate legislation is issued.

01. The new Special Act takes effect on 9 September 2027

Act No. 21901, the Special Act on the Maintenance and Support of Vacant Buildings, was enacted and promulgated on 8 September 2026.

It takes effect on 9 September 2027, one year after promulgation.[1]

From that date, rules previously dispersed across statutes governing vacant homes, other vacant buildings and stalled construction will be reorganised around the new Special Act.

02. Why a separate Vacant Buildings Special Act was enacted

The government’s legislative explanation notes that, as of 2024, South Korea had about 134,000 vacant homes and potentially about 61,000 other vacant buildings.[2]

Long-term vacancy can create collapse, fire and crime risks, damage the streetscape, depress nearby property values and accelerate neighbourhood decline.

In population-loss areas, this can become a cycle of depopulation, more vacancy, worsening living conditions and further population outflow.

The Act therefore moves beyond after-the-fact demolition and creates an integrated framework for survey, planning, maintenance, reuse and support.

03. The law is not limited to vacant homes

Under the previous framework, vacant homes, other vacant buildings and long-stalled construction sites could fall under different statutes.

The new Act brings qualifying residential, non-residential and stalled-construction properties into one category of “vacant buildings.”

It also creates a category of buildings subject to management for underused buildings that have not yet become vacant buildings but require preventive management or maintenance.

04. A home may qualify after at least one year of non-use

A residential building may qualify when the competent local authority confirms that no one has resided in or used it for at least one year.

Residential buildings are not subject to a separate 20-year age requirement.

Unsold housing and public administrative facilities, among other buildings to be prescribed by Presidential Decree, are excluded. The detailed exclusions must therefore be checked once subordinate legislation is issued.[1]

05. Non-residential buildings require 20 years since completion plus one year of non-use

The threshold differs for shops, offices and other non-residential buildings.

A non-residential building may qualify if it is at least 20 years old from completion and the local authority confirms that no one has used it for at least one year.

Age alone is not enough, and one year of non-use alone is not enough if the 20-year requirement is not met.[1]

06. Construction stalled for at least two years is also covered

Unfinished buildings can also fall within the Act.

A building under construction or major repair after a commencement report under the Building Act, or construction begun under the Housing Act, is treated as a stalled-construction building if the local authority confirms that work has been suspended for at least two years.

This brings long-abandoned construction sites into the same integrated management framework.[1]

07. The existing long-stalled construction statute will be repealed

When the new Act takes effect, the existing special statute on long-stalled construction will be repealed.

Vacant-home provisions are also separated from the Act on Special Cases Concerning Unoccupied House or Small-Scale Housing Redevelopment, whose title changes to the Act on Special Cases Concerning Small-Scale Housing Redevelopment.

Accordingly, from 9 September 2027 the core urban rules for vacant buildings and stalled construction will be reorganised around the new Act.[1][5][6]

08. Separate rural and fishing-village legislation may take priority

Not every vacant home is governed only by the new Act.

In certain eup and myeon areas defined under Korea’s agriculture and fisheries framework statutes, the Special Act on the Maintenance and Management of Vacant Houses in Rural and Fishing Villages generally takes priority.

The new Act may still apply where needed for matters such as surveys and basic maintenance planning.

The first practical question is therefore which statutory regime applies to the property’s location.[1][3]

09. Underused buildings may become buildings subject to management

A notable feature is that the Act does not wait until a building is fully vacant.

Underused buildings requiring preventive maintenance or management may qualify as buildings subject to management if statutory conditions are met.

This can include properties entered into the information system by owners seeking disposal or maintenance, or by local authorities for residential-environment or public-safety purposes.

Detailed eligibility criteria are delegated to Presidential Decree.

10. Full surveys every five years and status surveys every year

Local authorities must conduct a full survey every five years of vacant buildings and buildings presumed to be vacant.

The survey covers matters such as vacancy status, grade, maintenance history and administrative measures.

For stalled construction, it also covers construction progress, suspension periods and direct or indirect causes of the stoppage.

A separate annual status survey covers vacancy status, maintenance performance and administrative measures.

11. Owners are notified and may request re-verification

If an on-site check confirms a vacant building, the owner must be notified.

An owner who disputes the result may request re-verification within 14 days, and the authority must notify the owner of the re-verification result within 14 days after receiving the request.

If no request is made, or the building remains confirmed as vacant, it is entered into the statutory information system.

Owners should therefore treat a vacancy-classification notice as a substantive legal event rather than a routine administrative notice.

12. A nationwide vacant-building information system will be established

The Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport must establish and operate an information system for efficient vacant-building management.

Survey results, maintenance projects and administrative measures can thereby be managed systematically.

Even a building that does not qualify as vacant may be entered ex officio if maintenance or management is needed to improve the residential environment or protect residents.

Buildings presenting a significant risk of safety accidents, crime or sanitary harm must be entered into the system.

The framework therefore moves local vacancy policy toward data-based management.

13. A 10-year national framework and five-year regional plans

The Act creates a layered planning system.

The national government establishes a 10-year basic policy for vacant-building maintenance, with review where necessary every five years.

Provinces and metropolitan-level governments prepare five-year strategic plans, while local governments prepare and implement five-year basic maintenance plans.

Those plans address survey results, regional vacancy characteristics, promotion zones, maintenance projects, safety measures and financing.

14. Local basic plans require public participation

Local basic maintenance plans are subject to procedural safeguards.

As a rule, the local authority must consult the relevant higher-level local government, make the plan available for public inspection for at least 14 days, collect residents’ views and obtain joint review by the urban-planning and building committees.

The designation of promotion zones and maintenance strategy is therefore not an entirely internal administrative process.

15. Areas with concentrated vacancy may become maintenance-promotion zones

When establishing or amending a basic plan, local authorities may designate qualifying areas as vacant-building maintenance-promotion zones.

The Act identifies, among others, areas where the number of vacant buildings is increasing or the vacancy ratio is high.

Areas already undergoing certain maintenance projects under other statutes are excluded.

Detailed designation criteria are delegated to Presidential Decree.

16. Some promotion zones are deemed urban-regeneration activation areas

If a promotion zone satisfies the statutory requirements under the Special Act on the Promotion of and Support for Urban Regeneration, it is deemed designated as an urban-regeneration activation area without duplicating the full process.

This allows vacancy policy to connect with broader urban-regeneration projects rather than remaining limited to demolition or repair of individual buildings.

17. Building coverage and floor-area ratios may be relaxed up to 130% of statutory maxima

Designation as a promotion zone does not automatically increase building coverage or floor-area ratios to 130%.

Where a promotion zone satisfies requirements to be prescribed by Presidential Decree, the applicable limits may be relaxed up to 130% of the statutory maximum limits under Articles 77 and 78 of the National Land Planning and Utilization Act.[1][4]

The reference point is therefore the statutory maximum, not an automatic 130% multiplication of whatever ratio currently applies to a specific parcel.

Actual use of the relaxation will depend on subordinate legislation and the specific project plan.

18. Maintenance projects are not limited to demolition

Vacant-building maintenance projects can include interior works, rebuilding, extension, major repair, completion of stalled construction, change of use, demolition, new construction after demolition, and installation of infrastructure or community facilities.

Within promotion zones, the Act also allows links with other redevelopment, urban-development and urban-regeneration programmes.

19. Multiple public and private actors may implement projects

Projects may be implemented by local authorities or vacant-building owners.

Where appropriate, local authorities may also implement projects directly or designate entities such as the Korea Land and Housing Corporation, local public corporations, construction businesses, qualifying housing businesses, real-estate investment companies, social enterprises, cooperatives and non-profit corporations.

The system therefore contemplates redevelopment and regeneration involving public and private actors, not only owner-funded demolition.

20. Owners may request purchase by the local authority or project implementer

Local authorities and qualifying project implementers may acquire needed properties through agreement, expropriation, auction or public sale.

Conversely, a vacant-building owner may request purchase by the competent local authority or project implementer.

As a rule, the recipient must notify the owner whether it will purchase the property within 30 days.

Detailed purchase methods and procedures are delegated to Presidential Decree.

21. Urgent projects may be designated as leading projects

The Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport and metropolitan-level local governments may promote projects as leading projects where maintenance is urgent or the expected policy impact is substantial.

Administrative and financial support may be provided to participating project implementers.

22. Owners may be ordered to take safety measures

Local authorities must order vacant-building owners to take safety measures in accordance with the basic plan.

Measures may include preventing cracks, falling materials or collapse of exterior walls, roofs and retaining walls; removing stored combustible materials; managing fire, electricity and gas risks; closing entrances; installing safety fencing; and posting crime-prevention warnings.

A building not included in the basic plan may still become subject to safety measures after review by the building committee.

23. Demolition orders can become mandatory for high-risk buildings

Not every demolition order has the same legal character.

Where abandonment is inappropriate for the surrounding living environment or seriously harms the streetscape, the authority may order necessary measures including demolition.

Where there is a high risk of collapse or fire, a high risk of crime, or a sanitary-harm risk, the authority must order necessary measures including demolition.[1]

For these high-risk categories, the law therefore moves beyond administrative discretion to a statutory duty to act.

24. The standard compliance period is 60 days

An owner receiving a safety or demolition order must generally comply within 60 days, unless a special reason prescribed by Presidential Decree applies.

If compliance within 60 days is substantially difficult because of unavoidable circumstances such as disaster or technical difficulty, the period may be extended once for up to another 60 days.

The details of qualifying special reasons will need to be checked in subordinate legislation.

25. Unknown ownership does not prevent demolition after a 60-day public notice process

If the location of the owner cannot be determined, the process does not stop indefinitely.

The authority must announce the demolition order and the possibility of ex officio demolition at least once in the local government’s official gazette or website, and in a daily newspaper.

If the owner does not submit an opinion within 60 days from the announcement, the authority must carry out ex officio demolition.[1]

The statutory structure is “gazette or website” plus “daily newspaper,” not a mandatory publication in all three channels.

26. Ex officio demolition generally requires just compensation

Where a local authority demolishes a vacant building ex officio, it must generally pay the owner just compensation as prescribed by Presidential Decree.

Demolition costs may be deducted from the compensation.

If demolition costs exceed the compensation amount, the authority may collect the difference additionally as prescribed by Presidential Decree.[1]

Compensation may be deposited where the owner refuses payment, cannot be located or payment is prevented by attachment or provisional attachment.

The detailed valuation method must be confirmed once subordinate legislation is issued.

27. Urgent risks can justify bypassing ordinary administrative-execution steps

Where disaster risk is imminent, structural safety creates a serious collapse risk, road passage is materially obstructed or similar statutory conditions exist, ordinary administrative execution procedures may be too slow.

If the purpose cannot be achieved through the ordinary procedures under Article 3(1) and (2) of the Administrative Vicarious Execution Act, the new Act permits urgent execution without those steps.[1]

That power is not unlimited: the Act requires measures to remain within the minimum extent necessary for building management.

28. Repeated enforcement charges may apply until an order is obeyed

Failure to comply with a safety, demolition or other statutory order can trigger enforcement charges.

For completed vacant buildings, the base amount uses 50% of the standard value per square metre multiplied by total floor area, with a percentage prescribed by Presidential Decree depending on the order.

For stalled-construction buildings, the amount is set by local ordinance within a ceiling of KRW 10 million.

Taking into account the duration and reasons for noncompliance, the amount must be increased under local ordinance by up to 100% of the initially calculated charge.[1]

Charges may be imposed and collected up to twice per year until compliance, measured from the date of the first order.

This is therefore not a one-off administrative fine that ends the matter.

29. Public funding can support maintenance projects and surveys

The national and local governments may cover all or part of costs needed for vacant-building management and maintenance.

Support may include subsidies, investment or financing for maintenance projects, full and annual surveys, and other management or maintenance costs prescribed by Presidential Decree.

The Act therefore combines stronger owner obligations with public financing tools.

30. Metropolitan-level governments must establish vacant-building maintenance funds

Metropolitan-level local governments must establish and operate vacant-building maintenance funds to support maintenance projects.

Lower-level local governments may also establish funds where needed for projects within their jurisdictions.

Practical project activity will therefore depend not only on the statute but also on how local governments capitalise and deploy those funds.

31. Individual projects can receive building-regulation relaxations

The Act allows qualifying maintenance projects to receive regulatory relaxations as prescribed by Presidential Decree.

Potential areas include use restrictions by zoning district, building coverage and floor-area ratios, landscaping, building lines, open-space setbacks, building height, urban parks and green space, and certain ancillary or welfare-facility requirements.

The statute does not itself prescribe one uniform relaxation level for every project.

Detailed requirements must be checked against subordinate legislation and the individual project plan.

32. Older vacant buildings may be repurposed as ‘urban infill facilities’

Vacant buildings whose exterior can be preserved while fitting the surrounding streetscape may be designated as urban infill facilities after review by the local building committee.

The concept allows reuse rather than assuming that every old building must first be demolished and rebuilt.

For such facilities, the designating authority may determine use notwithstanding ordinary zoning-use restrictions under Article 76 of the National Land Planning and Utilization Act.[1][4]

However, harmful facilities and other uses prescribed by Presidential Decree are excluded, so the actual permissible-use envelope must be checked once subordinate legislation is issued.

33. Vacant buildings may also be used for emergency housing

Local governments may temporarily use qualifying vacant buildings for people requiring urgent housing support, including disaster victims and certain emergency-welfare recipients.

This can include buildings owned directly by the local government or properties made available under a lease or agreement with the owner.

The basic temporary-use period for privately owned property is six months, extendable by agreement with the owner.

Local governments may support all or part of necessary alteration, repair and maintenance costs.

34. A new vacant-building management business sector is formalised

The Act creates a regulated vacant-building management business.

It distinguishes a responsibility-based model, in which the operator leases and subleases or operates the property on its own responsibility, from an agency model, in which the operator receives fees to collect rent and manage facilities for the owner.

Operators must register with the competent local authority and satisfy statutory capital and professional-staff requirements.

Within statutory limits, management may include lease administration, rent collection, maintenance and repair, and changes to tenant business categories.

35. Existing vacant-home concentration zones generally transition into promotion zones

Vacant-home concentration zones designated under the previous vacant-home statute when the new Act takes effect are deemed vacant-building maintenance-promotion zones.

That status is not indefinite.

If an existing zone is not reflected in the basic maintenance plan prepared after the new Act takes effect, the promotion-zone designation is deemed revoked.[1][6]

The supplementary provisions also preserve, in principle, prior dispositions, procedures and acts by deeming them taken under the corresponding provisions of the new Act.

36. Owners should look beyond the yes-or-no question of vacancy

Owners should check not only whether a property is legally vacant but also whether it is a building subject to management, what survey and safety grades apply, whether it has been entered into the information system, and whether any safety or demolition order has been issued.

If the property lies in a promotion zone or project area, options such as sale, participation in a maintenance project, renovation, change of use or professional management should be considered alongside demolition.

37. A demolition order should not be left unanswered

Many statutory orders use a basic 60-day compliance period.

If compliance is genuinely impossible or technically difficult, owners should preserve objective evidence and assess whether an extension is available.

Doing nothing can lead to ex officio demolition and repeated enforcement charges.

For high-risk buildings, the authority itself may be under a duty to act, so delay based on the assumption that the local government will not move quickly is particularly risky.

38. Developers should examine area-wide incentives, not only the individual building

For developers, the acquisition price of one vacant building is only part of the analysis. Whether the property falls within a maintenance-promotion zone can materially affect the project.

Promotion zones can connect with urban regeneration, building coverage and floor-area ratio relief, and other project-structure rules.

Individual maintenance projects may also receive building-regulation relief and may use the urban infill facility mechanism for change of use.

Actual feasibility must still be tested against Presidential Decrees, local ordinances, basic plans and project-specific permitting conditions.

39. Subordinate legislation remains a major implementation issue

As of 16 November 2026, the Act has been promulgated but is not yet in force, and many delegated details remain to be specified in Presidential Decrees or ministerial rules.

These include exclusions from the vacant-building definition, detailed criteria for buildings subject to management, promotion-zone designation, conditions for the 130% planning relaxation, other building-regulation relaxations, special reasons for extending safety or demolition orders, compensation valuation, certain support and registration procedures, and excluded harmful uses for urban infill facilities.

It is therefore essential to distinguish rules already fixed by the statute from matters that remain for subordinate legislation.

40. The policy shift is from post-abandonment demolition to early management and reuse

The Act is not simply a measure to make demolition of long-vacant buildings easier.

It creates one framework under which homes unused for at least one year, qualifying non-residential buildings at least 20 years old and unused for at least one year, and construction stalled for at least two years can be surveyed and managed.

High-risk buildings may face safety orders, demolition, ex officio action and enforcement charges, while reusable buildings gain new options through maintenance support, regulatory relief, urban infill facilities and professional management.

For owners, developers and local governments, the practical question after 2027 will increasingly be not merely “Should this building be demolished?” but “When does management begin, and how should the building be maintained or reused?”
