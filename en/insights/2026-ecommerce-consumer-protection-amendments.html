South Korea’s 2026 E-Commerce Act Amendments — Private-Seller Information, Review Policies and Platform Liability

The amendments to South Korea’s Act on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce, Etc. (the “E-Commerce Act”), effective on 21 July 2026, revise information-verification and dispute-cooperation duties for private-party transaction intermediaries, consumer-review operating disclosures, consent decisions and temporary suspension orders.[1][2]

The amendment should not be understood as requiring all of a second-hand seller’s personal information to be disclosed to the buyer, or as prohibiting online shops from removing reviews. Distinguish who must verify which information, what request permits disclosure, and what must be published and actually implemented.

Key takeaways

  • Business sellers and private sellers have separate identity-information frameworks. Verification and disclosure following a private-party dispute require separate analysis.[1][3]
  • A court request and a buyer’s direct request have different conditions. A court request for investigation under Civil Procedure Act Article 294 should not be confused with a document-production order.[3][4]
  • Businesses publishing reviews must disclose four categories of operating information. Disclosure of a policy does not itself legitimise false reviews or deceptive operation.[5][7]
  • Withdrawal of a consent-decision application differs from revocation after a decision. Domestic-representative requirements and related administrative fines take effect separately on 21 January 2027.[8][9][12]

01. Distinguish the July Commencement, Review-Disclosure Guidance Period and Next Year’s Domestic-Representative Rules

The principal provisions of the E-Commerce Act amendment, promulgated on 20 January 2026 as Act No. 21312, took effect on 21 July 2026. The implementing amendment to the Enforcement Decree, Presidential Decree No. 36507, was promulgated on 20 July 2026, with its principal provisions likewise taking effect on 21 July.[1][2]

AreaCommencement or operational datePosition at the review date
Information verification and dispute cooperation for intermediated private-party transactions21 July 2026In force
Disclosure of consumer-review collection and handling information21 July 2026In force
Full enforcement of review-information disclosure requirementsFrom 22 October 2026, according to KFTC guidanceGuidance period announced
Introduction of consent decisions and revision of temporary suspension orders21 July 2026In force
Domestic representatives for certain overseas businesses and related sanctions21 January 2027Not yet in force

These dates follow the supplementary provisions to the Act and Enforcement Decree and the Korea Fair Trade Commission’s (KFTC) guidance on review-information disclosure.[1][2][6]

The review-disclosure obligation does not first arise in October

In its 7 September 2026 announcement of a question-and-answer guide, the KFTC explained that it would operate a three-month guidance period from 21 July and begin full application of the law on 22 October. This does not amend the statutory commencement date and should not be extended into a blanket enforcement deferral for the entire amended Act.[6]

Businesses should separate obligations already in force from those taking effect later and check the latest guidance-period announcements when responding in practice.

02. Separate Information-Verification Frameworks Apply to Business and Private Sellers

The amended Act retains identity verification and disclosure for sellers acting as businesses in Article 20(2), while separating the obligations relating to transactions by private sellers who are not businesses into Article 20-4.[1][3]

The Enforcement Decree correspondingly deletes former Article 25(2) and introduces Article 25-3 to specify the identity information to be verified for private sellers. The deleted provision is the former private-seller rule, not the entire identity-verification obligation for business sellers.[2]

AreaPrincipal provisions after amendmentReview focus
Verification and disclosure for business sellersArticle 20(2) of the Act; Article 25(1) of the DecreeIdentity information to be provided to consumers before they place an order
Verification of private sellers’ identity informationArticle 20-4(1) of the Act; Article 25-3 of the DecreeInformation categories and conditions tailored to private-party transactions
Disclosure after a private-party dispute arisesArticle 20-4(2) of the ActSeparate examination of the requester and statutory conditions

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

This is more than a renumbering exercise: the scope of verification and the routes for disclosure in a private-seller dispute have been separately revised.[1][2]

Information to Verify for Private Sellers and the Relevant Conditions

Article 25-3 of the Enforcement Decree specifies the following information.[2]

ItemInformationCondition
Item 1Telephone numberSubject to verification
Item 2Email addressApplies only where the intermediary does not hold the information under item 5
Item 3Identity information verified through an electronic-signature certification provider, credit information company or similar providerWhere held by the intermediary
Item 4Information on the private seller’s creditworthiness provided by the intermediaryWhere held by the intermediary
Item 5Identity information verified through an identity-verification agency designated under Article 23-3(1) of the Information and Communications Network ActWhere held by the intermediary

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Performing some form of identity check therefore does not automatically permit email verification to be omitted. The question is whether the intermediary holds information falling within Article 25-3, item 5 of the Decree. Items 3 to 5 likewise do not mean that every intermediary must newly collect all such information where it does not already hold it.[2]

In practice, distinguish information verified at registration, information already held and information that can be supplied when a dispute arises. An obligation to verify information and permission to disclose it to every user are separate matters.

03. Disclosure in a Dispute Depends on the Requester and Procedure

Article 20-4(2) requires an intermediary to cooperate in resolving a dispute between a private seller and the other party by supplying the verified identity information and transaction history when a qualifying statutory request is made.[3]

RequesterStatutory condition
Consumer-dispute mediation bodyA request by a body under Article 33 of the Act
CourtA request for the private seller’s identity information where required for proceedings under Article 294 of the Civil Procedure Act
Consumer who is the other party to the transactionThe private seller has consented to the intermediary providing identity information to that consumer

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

A buyer’s direct request is subject to the seller’s consent condition. The provisions governing requests by a court or a statutory mediation body do not contain the same condition. The three routes should not be treated identically.[3]

The Court Route Is a Request for Investigation, Not Necessarily a Document-Production Order

Article 294 of the Civil Procedure Act concerns a court request for investigation. It allows a court to request an institution, organisation or individual to make an investigation necessary in relation to its work, or to send transcripts or copies of documents in its custody. It is used in practice as a basis for court enquiries into facts and is distinct from the document-production procedures under Article 343 and the following provisions.[4]

The E-Commerce Act disclosure route should therefore not be described as requiring a court order to produce documents in every case. Equally, a party’s merely giving customer support a case number does not substitute for the court’s statutory request.

A practical process should verify the requesting court, the case, the legal basis, the transaction concerned and the scope of the requested information.

Not Every Consumer Rule Automatically Applies to Private-Party Transactions

Article 20-4 treats the other party to a private seller’s transaction as a consumer for the purposes of that Article only. That rule alone does not make the Act’s entire withdrawal framework automatically applicable to every private second-hand transaction.[3]

Escrow Must Be Explained and Recommended Where It Is Available

Article 20-4(3)1 requires private sellers and consumers to be informed of, and encouraged to use, the payment escrow contemplated by Article 13(2)10 where it is available. Article 20-4(3)2 requires appropriate identification of private sellers and business sellers where they are mixed on the service.[3]

The notification and recommendation duty should not be applied indiscriminately where that escrow arrangement is unavailable for the transaction. However, a platform not operating an escrow service itself and escrow being unavailable for the transaction are different facts. Examine how the service is supplied or connected and whether it can actually be used in the transaction concerned.

Information Cooperation and Joint and Several Liability Are Also Different

Article 20-2 of the E-Commerce Act provides for joint and several liability of an intermediary and seller in specified circumstances.[3]

ProvisionPrincipal conditions
Article 20-2(1)Where the intermediary failed to disclose that it was not a party to the transaction: pecuniary loss caused to a consumer by the seller’s intentional or negligent conduct
Article 20-2(2)Pecuniary loss caused to a consumer by failure to provide identity information under Article 20(2), or by providing information that is not true

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The exception for having exercised reasonable care is set out in the proviso to paragraph (2). It should not be presented as a common exemption applicable to every form of joint and several liability.[3]

After amendment, Article 20(2) concerns the identity-information framework for business sellers. Accordingly, a breach of Article 20-4 in a private-party transaction should not, by itself, be treated as automatically establishing liability under Article 20-2(2). The seller’s status, the applicable provision, loss and causation, and any separate contractual or damages liability require distinct examination.[3]

04. Businesses Publishing Consumer Reviews Must Disclose How They Operate Them

Article 21-4(1) requires an e-commerce business or mail-order seller to disclose review collection and handling information where it publishes consumer reviews of goods or services traded through an online marketplace or similar channel.[5]

The provision is not confined to large platforms. A business operating its own online shop should also examine whether it falls within the requirements.

Conversely, it does not require a business that publishes no consumer reviews to install a new review function. Because the trigger is publication, however, a business publishing consumer reviews should assess applicability even if there is no separate form through which consumers can write reviews. Neither a feature’s name nor its installation status is conclusive.[5]

Four Categories Are Specified Under the Act’s Delegation

Article 21-4(2) delegates the content and scope of the information and the disclosure methods and procedures to Presidential Decree. Article 27-3 of the Enforcement Decree specifies the following.[5]

Disclosure categoryWhat the operating policy should explain
Persons entitled to write reviewsWho may submit a review
Publication periodHow long reviews are published
Grading criteria and consequences of each gradeHow reviews are assessed and what effects the assessment has
Removal criteria and objection procedure following removalWhy a review may be removed and how an objection can be raised

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Disclosing who is entitled to write reviews is different from publishing an author’s personal identity, such as their name or contact information.[5]

The Starting Point Is the First Screen on Which Reviews Can Be Viewed

Article 27-3 requires disclosure so that consumers can view the information on the first screen on which they can see reviews. Where space is limited, it may be provided on a screen directly linked to that first screen.[5]

The mere existence of a policy document somewhere on a company website should therefore not be assumed sufficient. A useful practical check is to open the actual review screen on the mobile website, app and product page and follow the route to the policy.

05. Policy Disclosure and the Lawfulness of Review Handling Are Separate

The amendment does not prohibit every review removal. But publishing removal criteria does not make every removal lawful either. The information-disclosure requirement and potentially deceptive operation should be assessed separately.[5][7]

False Reviews and Selective Concealment May Engage a Separate Prohibition

Article 21(1)1 prohibits attracting consumers or conducting transactions through false or exaggerated statements or deceptive methods, as well as obstructing withdrawal or contract termination by such means.[7]

Posting fabricated content as if it were a genuine consumer review, or selectively concealing adverse information so as to misrepresent a product’s evaluation, may require examination under this prohibition. Not every removal or decision not to display a review is automatically unlawful. Identify what was handled, why it was handled that way and what impression was given to consumers.

In Supreme Court decision 2012Du1525, delivered on 26 June 2014, the Court addressed deceptive consumer inducement under the former law. It held that conduct liable to mislead consumers by concealing or minimising facts affecting purchasing decisions can suffice; proof that consumers were actually induced is not additionally required.[7]

That decision did not directly apply the newly introduced review-information provision. It should be treated as legal background for examining existing deception rules alongside the new disclosure obligation.

Connect the Objection Procedure to Actual Handling

Where a published policy describes an objection procedure that cannot actually be accessed, or customer support and automated systems apply different standards, amending the wording alone does not complete operational preparation.

A useful internal record links the review concerned, the policy version, the reason for removal, receipt of the objection, responsibility for reconsideration and communication of the result. This is a suggested operational check, not a prescribed statutory form.

Article 21-4 and Article 27-3 of the Decree do not prescribe a uniform 24-hour or 48-hour handling deadline. Any other applicable dispute-handling rules or contractual deadlines must nevertheless be checked separately.[5]

06. Manage the Application, Withdrawal and Implementation of a Consent Decision Separately

Article 32-2 allows a business subject to KFTC investigation or deliberation to apply for a consent decision intended to improve trading conditions and provide consumer redress. The application must set out the facts, proposed corrective measures and measures to remedy or prevent harm in writing.[8]

The KFTC may make a consent decision after assessing matters including whether the proposals are balanced against the sanctions expected if an infringement were found and are appropriate for restoring fair trading conditions or protecting consumers and other businesses. Filing an application does not produce automatic approval.[8]

Withdrawing the Application Before a Decision Leads to Deliberation Under the Act

The proviso to Article 32-2(1) requires the KFTC, where the applicant withdraws the application before a consent decision is made, not to make that decision and instead to proceed with deliberation under the Act.

Withdrawal should therefore not be mistaken for termination of the underlying case concerning the conduct under investigation.[8]

A Consent Decision Is Not a Finding of Infringement

Under Article 32-2(4), a consent decision does not mean that the conduct has been recognised as violating the Act. The existence of a consent decision should not be described as establishing an infringement.[8]

Nor should it be taken as automatically resolving every civil dispute or liability. The relationship between the actual corrective or redress measures and individual claims needs separate examination.

Withdrawal of an Application Differs from Revocation of an Existing Decision

Article 32-2(5) applies Articles 90 and 91 of the Monopoly Regulation and Fair Trade Act to the consent-decision procedure and revocation.[8][9]

The incorporated Article 91 permits revocation in circumstances such as a material change in the facts making the corrective measures inappropriate, a decision obtained through incomplete or inaccurate information or other improper means, or failure to comply without a justifiable reason. Depending on the ground for revocation, resumption of the suspended deliberation procedure may also arise.[9]

Plan for Enforcement Charges and Implementation Monitoring

Article 32-3 of the E-Commerce Act permits an enforcement charge of up to KRW 2 million per day where the implementation deadline is missed without a justifiable reason. It concerns the period after the deadline until the consent decision is implemented or revoked; it does not mean that KRW 2 million is automatically charged for every day in every case.[8]

Article 34-2 of the Decree entrusts the Korea Consumer Agency with checking implementation of the plan and requesting relevant material. Businesses should identify the refund or corrective-action population, responsible personnel, timetable and evidence of implementation from the application stage.[2][8]

07. Temporary Suspension Orders and Administrative Fines Have Separate Conditions

Not Every E-Commerce Act Infringement Qualifies for a Temporary Suspension Order

Revised Article 32-4 permits an order to stop the conduct concerned or temporarily suspend all or part of the e-commerce or mail-order business. Both of the following conditions must be satisfied.[10]

ConditionMatter to establish
Clear prohibited conductWhether the conduct is clearly considered to fall within Article 21(1)1 of the Act
Urgent need to prevent harmWhether there is an urgent need to prevent the spread of irreparable harm to numerous consumers

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Article 21(1)1 covers not only attracting consumers or conducting transactions through false, exaggerated or deceptive methods, but also obstructing withdrawal or contract termination. The mere receipt of a complaint or breach of another provision does not establish all the above conditions.[7][10]

The KFTC may request necessary measures from hosting-service providers, mail-order intermediaries and others to implement the temporary suspension. Recipients must comply unless they have a justifiable reason not to. A contact process is needed to establish the subject and scope of the order or cooperation request.[10]

Statutory Fine Ceilings Differ from the Amount Actually Imposed

The principal administrative fines already in force at the reference date are as follows.[11]

Failure to provide identity information and transaction history when the statutory conditions applyArticle 45(4), item 6-3KRW 10 million
Failure to notify and recommend available escrow or distinguish seller categoriesArticle 45(4), item 6-4KRW 10 million
Failure to disclose review collection and handling informationArticle 45(4), item 8KRW 10 million
Breach of the refund obligation under Article 18(2)Article 45(3), item 1-2KRW 20 million
Breach of a temporary suspension orderArticle 45(1)KRW 100 million

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The provision numbers and amounts above are specified in the Act. The amount actually imposed must be assessed under the Decree’s applicable criteria and other relevant rules.[11]

In particular, the refund entry does not require a refund whenever someone requests one. The initial question is whether a refund obligation under Article 18(2) has arisen.

Administrative fines, enforcement charges for failure to implement a consent decision, and a consumer’s refund or damages claim are separate mechanisms. An administrative sanction does not automatically transfer compensation to an individual consumer.

08. Domestic Representatives for Overseas Businesses and Related Sanctions Begin in 2027

The domestic-representative framework under Article 20-5 of the Act and Article 25-4 of the Decree takes effect on 21 January 2027. The related administrative-fine provisions take effect on the same date.[1][2][12]

First Check the Absence of a Korean Address or Place of Business

The framework covers mail-order sellers or mail-order intermediaries with no address or place of business in Korea that meet any one of the following criteria.[12]

CriterionPrincipal requirements
Total revenueTotal revenue of at least KRW 1 trillion in the preceding year; the preceding business year for a corporation
Domestic consumer scaleAt least one million domestic consumers per month on average accessing the online marketplace operated by the business during the three months preceding the end of the prior year
Receipt of a specified orderReceipt of a statutory order to report or submit material or items where an infringement has caused, or is likely to cause, significant consumer harm

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

The domestic-consumer criterion is a monthly average. It should not be confused with daily-average user thresholds under other legislation.[12]

The Representative’s Duties Differ for Sellers and Intermediaries

Article 20-5(1) distinguishes the matters to be handled by the representative as follows.[12]

Overseas business’s rolePrincipal matters handled by the domestic representative
Mail-order sellerOf the transaction terms to be performed under Article 13(5), performance of those concerning Article 13(2)8
Mail-order intermediaryNecessary measures to resolve consumer complaints and disputes under Article 20(3)
Common mattersCompliance with reporting and material/item-submission dispositions under Article 81(1)3 of the Monopoly Regulation and Fair Trade Act, as applied through Article 39(2)

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Article 13(2)8 concerns compensation for consumer harm, complaints about goods or services, and the handling of disputes between consumers and businesses. The representative should not be described as assuming every transaction term or all of the seller’s liabilities beyond that provision’s scope.[12]

The domestic representative must have an address or place of business in Korea. Where the overseas business has established a Korean corporation or exercises a controlling influence over one, it must select the representative from among those corporations. An effective means of communication with the overseas business must also be secured.[12]

Check Post-Appointment Filing, Disclosure and Sanctions Together

Article 25-4(3) of the Decree requires the prescribed representative information to be submitted to the KFTC in writing without delay after appointment or a change, and disclosed on the first screen of the online marketplace or similar service operated by the business.[12]

The related administrative fines taking effect on 21 January 2027 are as follows.[11][12]

ViolationProvisionStatutory ceiling
Failure to appoint a domestic representativeArticle 45(3), item 1-3KRW 20 million
Appointment contrary to statutory requirementsItem 1-4 of the same paragraphKRW 20 million
Failure to submit or disclose representative informationItem 1-5 of the same paragraphKRW 20 million
Failure to submit information, or submission of false information, for determining whether appointment is requiredItem 1-6 of the same paragraphKRW 20 million

These provisions are not yet in force at the reference date. Their commencement must be distinguished from fines for breaches of review-disclosure requirements and other obligations already in force.[11][12]

09. LAW NOAH Analysis — Map Transactions and Information Handling First

The following are practical suggestions for implementing the amendments, not a statement that the law requires every business to use the same form.

1) Identify Roles Transaction by Transaction Rather Than Relying on the Label “Platform”

One service may combine direct sales, intermediation for business sellers and intermediation for private-party transactions.

For each product or transaction type, identify the seller, intermediary, review operator, payment/refund personnel and recipient of dispute requests. Then connect each role with the relevant verification, disclosure, dispute-handling and liability rules.

In particular, do not end the assessment with a “private seller” label on screen. Check whether the seller’s actual status matches the procedures being applied.

2) Separate Request-Handling Routes Instead of Merely Collecting More Personal Information

Holding more information does not, on its own, complete dispute preparation. A process should distinguish a buyer’s direct request, a statutory mediation body’s request and a court request for investigation.

A possible workflow is:

Receive the request → verify the requester and legal basis → identify the transaction → establish the disclosure scope → check any required consent → record the outcome and grounds

The objective is to avoid subjecting a court request and a party’s request to the same consent process, or disclosing everything merely because there is a dispute. Record-retention periods and access rights should also be defined separately in light of the purpose of the information.

3) Connect Published Policies to Actual Decisions

Review policies are not completed simply by drafting text. Screen implementation, customer-support guidance and automated grading/removal functions should apply consistent criteria.

For individual decisions, consider retaining a record of the applicable policy version, reason, handling time, objection and reconsideration outcome.

Consumers facing a transaction or review dispute should likewise organise contemporaneous screenshots, order and payment records, enquiries, responses and the published policy. This helps identify precisely what was requested and why it was refused.

Responding to this amendment does not mean indiscriminately collecting more personal information or stopping all review removals.

The priority is to distinguish the counterparty’s status, the lawful disclosure route, review-operation criteria and post-dispute responsibilities and measures, and make those standards consistent with actual screens and workflows.
