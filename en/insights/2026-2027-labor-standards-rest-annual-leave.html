South Korea’s 2026–2027 Labor Standards Act Amendments — Four-Hour Rest-Break Choice and Split Annual Leave

South Korea’s Labor Standards Act will first create a narrow exception to the 30-minute break rule for exactly four hours of work, then introduce an express statutory right to request annual leave in smaller units and prohibit adverse treatment for taking annual leave.

Key points

  • From 10 December 2026, an employee working exactly four hours may expressly request not to use the statutory 30-minute break.
  • From 10 June 2027, an express statutory right to request split annual leave takes effect.
  • The current draft Enforcement Decree proposes half-day units and up to five days per year, but this is not yet final.
  • Failure to grant a qualifying statutory split-leave request can trigger Article 110 criminal penalties.
  • Adverse treatment for requesting or using annual leave will also be expressly prohibited.

01. One amendment, but two different effective dates

Act No. 21784 was promulgated on 9 June 2026.

Most of the amendment takes effect on 10 June 2027, one year after promulgation.

The Article 54 rest-break amendment takes effect six months earlier, on 10 December 2026.[1]

Effective datePrincipal change
10 December 2026Exception to the rest-break requirement for an employee working exactly four hours
10 June 2027Statutory split annual-leave requests and prohibition on adverse treatment for annual-leave use

It is therefore inaccurate to describe both changes simply as rules that begin in 2027.

02. Under the current rule, four hours of work requires at least a 30-minute break

Article 54(1) of the Labor Standards Act requires at least 30 minutes of break time during four hours of work and at least one hour during eight hours of work.

The break must be provided during working time and must be freely usable by the employee.

Accordingly, even where an employee preferred to work four hours continuously and leave immediately, the statutory break could not simply be waived by agreement.

Article 54 is backed by criminal sanctions: a violation can be punishable by up to two years’ imprisonment or a fine of up to KRW 20 million.[1]

03. From 10 December, an exception applies to exactly four hours of work

From 10 December 2026, a new proviso to Article 54(1) creates a limited exception.

The exception applies where the working time is exactly four hours and the employee expressly requests not to use the break time.[1]

If both conditions are met, the employer may operate the schedule without the otherwise mandatory 30-minute break.

04. The employee’s express request is essential

The amendment does not permit an employer to abolish rest breaks unilaterally.

The statute expressly requires that the employee make an explicit request not to use the break.[1]

A blanket company rule stating that all four-hour workers will work without breaks would therefore not satisfy the statutory condition by itself.

05. The Act does not prescribe a specific form for the request

The amendment does not say that the request must be made on paper, electronically, or separately for every shift.

For dispute-prevention purposes, however, employers should consider retaining a written or electronic record showing the employee’s request, the date, the period for which it applies, the relevant working hours and any later withdrawal or change.

A standard clause inserted by the employer into every employment contract is not necessarily the same as a genuine express request by the employee.

If an employer removes the break without the employee’s request, Article 54 and its criminal sanction remain relevant.[1]

06. A request does not automatically create a statutory right to leave 30 minutes earlier

The amendment does not say that an employer must abolish the break whenever an employee asks.

Rather, it creates an exception to the employer’s statutory duty to provide the 30-minute break where the conditions are met.

The employee’s request therefore does not by itself create an independent statutory right to leave work 30 minutes earlier. Actual working and finishing times must also be assessed under the employment contract, work rules, scheduling arrangements and any agreement between the parties.

07. The exception is limited to exactly four hours

The new proviso applies where the working time is four hours.

It should not be extended to shifts exceeding four hours.

If actual working time goes beyond four hours, the employer must reassess the statutory rest-break requirement rather than relying on an earlier no-break request.

08. The rest-break rule also matters in workplaces with fewer than five employees

It is incorrect to assume that the Labor Standards Act never applies to workplaces with fewer than five employees.

Appendix 1 to the Enforcement Decree applies Article 54 to workplaces that ordinarily employ four or fewer employees.[2]

The December 2026 four-hour rest-break exception is therefore relevant to small restaurants, cafés, retailers and other businesses that use short part-time shifts.

09. Employers should update both the request process and timekeeping

Compliance requires more than inserting one sentence into work rules.

Employers should determine which employees actually work exactly four hours, how a no-break request is made and recorded, how long it remains effective, how it may be withdrawn, how it appears in schedules and time records, and what happens when actual work unexpectedly exceeds four hours.

10. In 2027, a new Article 60(5) will be inserted

Under the current Act, Article 60(5) contains the employee’s right to designate annual-leave timing and the employer’s limited right to change that timing.

From 10 June 2027, the paragraph structure changes.

The amendment moves the current paragraphs 5 through 7 to paragraphs 6 through 8 and inserts a new paragraph 5.[1]

The new paragraph requires an employer to grant annual leave when an employee requests to divide leave under paragraphs 1, 2 or 4 within the units of time and number of days to be prescribed by Presidential Decree.

After the amendment, paragraph 5 will govern split-leave requests and paragraph 6 will contain the timing rule and the employer’s timing-change power.

11. Split annual leave is not a concept appearing for the first time in 2027

Many employers already operate half-day, quarter-day or hourly-leave systems.

In a 2024 statutory interpretation, the Ministry of Government Legislation also concluded that where an employee requested annual leave in two-hour units, the employer could not change the requested timing for reasons other than the statutory requirement of substantial interference with business operations.[4]

The 2027 reform should therefore not be described as the first moment at which annual leave can ever be split.

Its significance is that the Act will expressly establish a split-leave request right and require a minimum mandatory scope to be defined by Presidential Decree.

12. A universal one-hour annual-leave right has not been fixed

The Act itself does not state the exact minimum unit.

The new Article 60(5) delegates the relevant time unit and number of days to Presidential Decree.[1]

It is therefore inaccurate, at this stage, to say that every employee will have a statutory right to use annual leave one hour at a time.

13. The current draft Enforcement Decree proposes half-days and five days per year

On 13 July 2026, the Ministry of Employment and Labor published a draft amendment to the Enforcement Decree.

The draft proposes a mandatory split-use range of half-day units and up to five days per year.[3]

If adopted unchanged, the statutory minimum unit would therefore be a half-day rather than one hour.

As of 24 November 2026, however, this remains a draft. The final Enforcement Decree must be checked before the 2027 provisions take effect.

14. ‘Five days per year’ does not reduce total annual leave to five days

The draft’s five-day figure is not a cap on the employee’s total annual-leave entitlement.

Annual-leave accrual continues to be calculated under the existing Article 60 framework.

The proposal concerns the portion for which the employer would be legally required to permit split use.[3]

Employers may continue to provide more generous half-day or hourly systems through work rules, agreements or practice.

15. The rule also covers leave for employees with less than one year of service and long-service additional leave

The new Article 60(5) refers to leave under paragraphs 1, 2 and 4: ordinary annual leave after 80% attendance, leave accruing for employees with less than one year of service or less than 80% attendance, and additional leave for long service.[1]

The split-request mechanism is therefore not limited to employees receiving the standard 15-day annual entitlement.

Whether the employee is entitled to annual leave in the first place must still be assessed separately.

16. Workplaces with fewer than five employees and ultra-short-hour workers require separate analysis

The coverage rules for rest breaks and annual leave are different.

Article 54 applies to workplaces with four or fewer employees, while Article 60 generally does not.[2]

In addition, Article 60 does not apply to an employee whose prescribed weekly working hours average less than 15 hours over four weeks.

That threshold cannot always be determined merely from the label used in the contract.

In a 2024 case involving university lecturers, the Supreme Court held that prescribed working time could not be judged solely by classroom teaching hours and required consideration of the time ordinarily needed for teaching preparation, student management, evaluation and other contractual duties.[5]

17. Split leave must generally be granted at the time requested by the employee

Split leave under the new paragraph 5 will also be subject to the amended paragraph 6.

As a rule, the employer must grant annual leave at the time requested by the employee.

The timing may be changed only where granting leave at that time would cause substantial interference with business operations.[1]

The Ministry of Government Legislation has similarly concluded under the current law that administrative inconvenience or the absence of an internal hourly-leave system is not enough by itself to change the employee’s requested timing.[4]

The timing-change power is therefore an exceptional power to adjust timing, not a general right to extinguish or categorically reject annual leave.

In practice, an employer changing the requested timing should identify and discuss a concrete alternative rather than simply recording the request as “denied.”

18. Failure to grant a statutory split-leave request can carry criminal sanctions

The amendment also updates the penalty provision.

Current Article 110 covers violations of Article 60(1), (2), (4) and (5).

The amendment expands the reference to Article 60(1), (2), and (4) through (6).[1]

Accordingly, after 10 June 2027, failure to comply with a qualifying split-leave request under the new paragraph 5, or a violation of paragraph 6, can be punishable by up to two years’ imprisonment or a fine of up to KRW 20 million.

Employers should therefore update HR systems once the final Enforcement Decree fixes the mandatory split-use range.

19. Adverse treatment for requesting or using annual leave will also be prohibited

The amendment adds Article 60(9).

An employer may not dismiss or otherwise treat an employee adversely because the employee requested or used annual paid leave.[1]

Amended Article 114 expressly adds violations of Article 60(9) to the offences punishable by a fine of up to KRW 5 million.[1]

The issue can therefore extend beyond formal approval of the leave request to retaliation through evaluation, placement, promotion or other employment treatment.

20. The reform does not create more annual leave, and the leave-promotion system remains

The amendment does not increase the basic number of annual-leave days.

The existing accrual structure—15 days after 80% attendance, monthly accrual for certain employees with less than one year of service, and long-service additions—remains.

The Supreme Court has also explained that the right to annual leave or annual-leave pay generally arises as consideration for work performed during the preceding year.[6]

The 2027 reform instead clarifies the statutory method for dividing leave that has already accrued.

The annual-leave promotion mechanism under Article 61 also remains. The amendment adjusts cross-references to reflect the renumbering of Article 60; it does not abolish the promotion system or create a separate new class of annual leave.[1]

21. Substituting annual leave and splitting annual leave are different mechanisms

Article 62 allows an employer, based on a written agreement with the employee representative, to substitute specified working days for annual paid leave.

That mechanism differs from the individual employee’s split-leave request right introduced in 2027.

Split leave concerns an employee dividing his or her own annual leave within the statutory range. Leave substitution concerns replacing specified working days with annual-leave days under a written representative agreement.

An existing leave-substitution arrangement therefore does not eliminate the need to consider an individual employee’s statutory split-leave rights.

22. Employers already operating half-day or hourly leave should review their rules

Many workplaces already allow half-day, quarter-day or hourly annual leave through work rules or HR systems.

From 2027, the Act and Enforcement Decree will establish a statutory minimum split-use standard, so employers should confirm that existing systems do not fall below that floor.

Items to review include the minimum unit, maximum split-use days, request timing and procedure, timing-change process, remaining-leave calculations, interaction with leave promotion, leave substitution under a representative agreement, HR and payroll systems, and safeguards against adverse treatment.

If an existing policy is more favourable to employees than the final statutory minimum, the amendment does not itself require the employer to scale that policy back.

23. The four-hour break exception and split annual leave are legally different rights

The amendment contains two mechanisms with different legal structures.

The four-hour rest-break rule creates a limited exception to the employer’s statutory duty where the employee requests it.

The split annual-leave rule requires the employer to grant a qualifying request within the statutory and regulatory range.

The former is an exception to a rest-break obligation; the latter is an express statutory split-leave request right.

They should not be treated as one generic working-time reduction policy.

24. What employers should check before the effective dates

Before 10 December 2026

Employers using exactly four-hour shifts should review the no-break request process, how requests are recorded and retained, actual working-time tracking, treatment of work exceeding four hours, and relevant employment-contract or work-rule provisions.

Before 10 June 2027

Employers subject to Article 60 should review the final split-use unit and number of days in the Enforcement Decree, existing half-day and hourly systems, request and timing-change procedures, interaction with annual-leave promotion and leave substitution, HR and payroll systems, and safeguards against adverse treatment.

The currently proposed half-day / five-days-per-year rule remains a draft and should not be treated as final until subordinate legislation is promulgated.

LAW NOAH Analysis — more choice for four-hour breaks, and an express statutory framework for split annual leave

The reform should not be reduced to slogans such as “four-hour workers no longer need breaks” or “everyone gets half-day leave in 2027.”

The rest-break amendment creates a narrow employee-driven exception for exactly four hours of work. The annual-leave amendment, by contrast, takes split-use practices that already existed in company policy and statutory interpretation and places an express request right and employer duty in the Act, with the minimum guaranteed scope to be fixed by Presidential Decree.

Employers should therefore implement the two reforms through separate procedures reflecting their different statutory conditions.
