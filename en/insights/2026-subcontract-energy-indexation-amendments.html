South Korea’s 2026 Fair Subcontract Transactions Act Amendments — Energy-Cost Indexation and Stronger Construction Payment Guarantees

From 11 August 2026, major energy costs are included in the subcontract-payment indexation regime, and exemptions from construction subcontract payment guarantees are substantially narrowed.

Key points

  • The amendments have been in force since 11 August 2026.
  • Energy used to manufacture, repair, construct or perform the contracted work may be a “major energy” input where its cost is at least 10% of the subcontract price.
  • The parties may set an indexation trigger, but it must be 10% or less.
  • The written terms must specify the energy input, benchmark, formula, reference/comparison points, adjustment date, cycle and payment-reflection date.
  • Non-indexation agreements are possible, but abuse of bargaining position and sham avoidance are prohibited.
  • Construction subcontract payment-guarantee exemptions are now generally limited to work priced at KRW 10 million or less.

01. The amendments have been in force since 11 August 2026

Act No. 21340 was promulgated on 10 February 2026 and took effect on 11 August 2026.[1]

The supplementary provisions apply the new major-energy indexation rules and revised construction-payment guarantee rules to subcontract agreements entered into or renewed after commencement.

02. What subcontract-payment indexation means

Subcontract-payment indexation is a contractual mechanism that predetermines how changes in specified cost inputs will be reflected in the subcontract price.

Article 2(18) defines indexation as adjustment of the subcontract price when the price of a major raw material or the cost of a major energy input changes by a threshold agreed by the parties within a range not exceeding 10%.[2]

03. What changed

The statutory regime previously focused on major raw materials. The amendment adds major energy costs.

The statutory risk-allocation framework therefore expands from major raw-material prices to major raw-material prices plus major-energy costs.

05. What is a “major energy” input?

Article 2(17) defines major energy as energy used in manufacturing, repair, construction or service performance for the contracted subject matter where the relevant energy cost is at least 10% of the subcontract price.[2]

The statutory reference is to energy under Article 2(1) and (3) of the Energy Act.

06. Which forms of energy are covered?

The Energy Act defines energy to include fuel, heat and electricity, and separately addresses new and renewable energy.[3]

The fuel definition excludes materials used as product inputs. Accordingly, industrial electricity, gas or oil used as a heat source and heat or steam may constitute energy, while a substance incorporated into the product itself may instead need to be analysed as a raw material.

07. The core calculation — energy cost attributable to the relevant contract

A useful conceptual calculation is: energy-cost share = energy cost attributable to the relevant subcontract ÷ subcontract price × 100.

The relevant figure is not necessarily the facility’s total utility bill; it is the cost attributable to performance of the specific subcontracted work.

08. Cost allocation is possible even without product-level metering

Where one facility produces multiple products or performs multiple contracts, the company must allocate energy costs to the relevant contract.

The Fair Trade Commission and Ministry of SMEs and Startups practical guide discusses approaches based on cost estimates, actual usage, production or operating measures, company-wide overhead ratios and financial-settlement data.[5]

The absence of product-level metering therefore does not necessarily make calculation impossible, but the allocation method should reasonably reflect actual energy consumption.

09. When should the 10% share be assessed?

Article 2(17) does not prescribe a single statutory measurement date.

Because the parties must determine the indexation terms at the contracting stage, it is important to assess major-energy status using objective information available when the contract is entered into or renewed, and to retain supporting records. A material change in the contract or cost structure may justify a fresh review.

10. Can different energy sources be aggregated for the 10% test?

The currently available official materials do not warrant an overbroad categorical statement on aggregation of different energy types.

Where several energy sources are used, the parties should document usage, allocation, benchmarks and formulas with sufficient specificity. Even where a statutory major-energy threshold is not met, the parties remain free to adopt voluntary indexation terms.

11. What must the written contract contain?

Article 3(2) of the Enforcement Decree requires the written indexation terms to identify:[4]

  1. the subject matter covered by indexation;
  2. the major raw material and major energy input;
  3. the adjustment trigger;
  4. the benchmark for raw-material prices and major-energy costs;
  5. the indexation formula;
  6. the reference and comparison points for measuring change;
  7. the adjustment date, adjustment cycle and date on which the adjusted price is reflected.

12. Two different 10% thresholds must be distinguished

The first 10% test asks whether the energy cost is at least 10% of the subcontract price, which determines major-energy status.

The second 10% figure is the maximum permissible adjustment trigger. The parties may agree on 3%, 5% or 10%, for example, but not 15% or 20%.

13. The benchmark and formula must actually work

The Act does not impose one formula for every industry. Parties may consider published electricity tariffs, applicable gas supply charges, reliable fuel-price data or actual purchase prices, depending on the contract.

The benchmark should reasonably track the energy cost actually borne under the subcontract. It is also prudent to address how a replacement benchmark will be selected if the original benchmark is discontinued or materially changed.

14. The formula should be designed for the contract

If, for example, electricity represents 15% of a KRW 100 million subcontract and the parties use a ±5% adjustment trigger with quarterly comparisons, the agreed formula can determine the price adjustment when the benchmark crosses that threshold.

A clause stating only that “the parties will discuss the price if energy costs increase” is much less useful than a clause specifying the benchmark, comparison date, formula and effective deliveries.

15. The four statutory exclusions remain important

Article 3(4) allows the indexation terms to be omitted in specified cases:[2]

  • the prime contractor is a statutory small enterprise;
  • the subcontract term is currently 90 days or less, subject to a different FTC notice for particular trading characteristics;
  • the subcontract price is currently KRW 100 million or less, likewise subject to an FTC notice;
  • the parties agree not to apply indexation, in which case the purpose and reason for the non-indexation agreement must be clearly recorded in writing.

16. A non-indexation agreement is not a blanket waiver

Article 3(5) prohibits a prime contractor from abusing its bargaining position or using false or other improper methods to avoid Article 3’s indexation requirements.[2]

Article 20(2) makes this special anti-evasion provision applicable ahead of the Act’s general anti-circumvention rule. A signed non-indexation form is therefore not a safe harbour if the agreement was coerced or based on sham reasons.

17. A purely formal indexation clause is not safe

Article 3(3) requires good-faith consultation over indexation terms.

Terms that adjust only when prices fall, use a cycle longer than the contract itself, rely on irrelevant benchmarks or effectively prevent the formula from reflecting cost changes may conflict with the purpose of the regime.[6]

18. How are existing contracts treated?

The supplementary provisions apply the major-energy amendments to subcontract agreements entered into or renewed on or after 11 August 2026.[1]

Pre-commencement agreements that continue without renewal are not simply retroactively rewritten by the amendment, but renewed contracts should be reviewed under the new regime.

19. Writing the clause is only the beginning

Operational compliance requires a process of checking the benchmark → calculating the change → testing the trigger → applying the formula → calculating the adjustment → paying the adjusted price.

The regime should not be treated as requiring a new discretionary request from the subcontractor every time if the parties have already agreed an automatic indexation mechanism.

20. The consequences extend beyond administrative fines

Failure to include required indexation terms may attract an administrative fine of up to KRW 10 million, while abusive or deceptive avoidance may attract a fine of up to KRW 50 million.[2]

Failure to pay an amount due under the agreed indexation terms may, depending on the facts, raise separate issues under the payment-duty and unlawful-reduction provisions, with potential criminal and administrative-surcharge exposure.

21. The same amendment sharply narrows construction payment-guarantee exemptions

Article 13-2 now generally requires the prime contractor in a construction subcontract to provide a payment guarantee within 30 days after contract formation, with the principal exemption narrowed to contracts worth KRW 10 million or less.[7]

Immediately before the amendment, the Enforcement Decree also recognised exemptions for specified direct-payment arrangements and electronic subcontract-payment management. Those decree-based exemptions were removed.

22. Direct-payment agreements no longer automatically remove the guarantee duty

Before the 2026 amendment, a qualifying direct-payment agreement among the project owner, prime contractor and subcontractor could constitute a payment-guarantee exemption.

That exemption has now been deleted. A direct-payment agreement therefore does not by itself eliminate the construction payment-guarantee duty under the current regime.

23. A guarantee duty may arise later if an exemption disappears

Article 13-2(2) requires the prime contractor to provide the payment guarantee within 30 days after an exemption ceases to apply.

The Enforcement Decree provides an exception where the remaining amount on the relevant construction contract is KRW 10 million or less at that time.[4]

24. Failure to provide the payment guarantee may also restrict enforcement of performance security

Article 13-2(9) permits the subcontractor not to provide performance security where the prime contractor has failed to provide the required payment guarantee. Paragraph (10) also restricts the prime contractor’s right to call the subcontractor’s performance security until the payment guarantee has been provided.

In Supreme Court case 2020Da296451, the Court held that late provision of the payment guarantee does not automatically bar a later performance-security claim. But where the prime contractor had failed to provide the guarantee for a substantial period and later provided it merely as a formal step to call performance security when default was imminent, the performance-security claim could be denied in the exceptional circumstances.[8]

25. Breaching the payment-guarantee duty carries separate sanctions

Failure to comply with Article 13-2 may expose the prime contractor to criminal and administrative sanctions under the Fair Subcontract Transactions Act.

It may also affect the contractor’s own practical ability to rely on performance-security protections.

26. Checklist for prime contractors

  • Identify contracts entered into or renewed after 11 August 2026.
  • Map major raw materials and energy-cost structures contract by contract.
  • Maintain objective evidence for allocating energy costs to the relevant contract.
  • Use a reasonable allocation method where product-level metering is unavailable.
  • Confirm that the energy falls within the statutory Energy Act categories.
  • State the major energy input and benchmark in the contract.
  • Set the trigger at no more than 10%.
  • Specify reference/comparison points, cycle and reflection date.
  • Provide for replacement benchmarks where appropriate.
  • Ensure non-indexation reasons reflect genuine negotiation.
  • Actually monitor benchmark movements and pay adjusted prices.
  • Re-test construction payment-guarantee duties under the amended rules.
  • Do not rely on a direct-payment agreement alone to omit the guarantee.
  • Revisit the guarantee requirement after amendments to the construction contract.

27. Checklist for subcontractors

  • Retain actual electricity, gas, fuel and heat-cost records.
  • Be able to explain how energy cost is attributed to the specific subcontract.
  • Check the 10% major-energy threshold.
  • Confirm that the benchmark reflects actual cost exposure.
  • Check that the trigger does not exceed 10%.
  • Review whether the adjustment cycle is excessively long.
  • Check whether the formula effectively neutralises cost increases.
  • Confirm that any non-indexation agreement was genuinely negotiated.
  • Verify that adjusted amounts are actually paid when the trigger is met.
  • For construction subcontracts, verify that the required payment guarantee has been provided.

28. LAW NOAH Analysis — cost-risk allocation must now be designed at contract formation

The significance of the amendment is not merely that electricity and gas have been added as another cost category. The deeper change is that subcontract agreements must more deliberately allocate external cost risks that neither party can fully control.

The useful contractual question is not “will we negotiate later if energy prices rise?” but “which energy cost is measured using which data, what benchmark movement triggers an adjustment, what formula applies, and from which deliveries does the new price take effect?”

Construction subcontracts add a second layer: the stricter payment-guarantee regime. Subcontract management after the 2026 amendment is therefore increasingly a cross-functional risk-management task involving procurement, costing, finance, production, site operations and legal.
