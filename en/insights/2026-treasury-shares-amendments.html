South Korea’s 2026 Commercial Act Treasury-Share Reform — One-Year Cancellation Rule, Annual Shareholder Approval and M&A Restrictions

Since 6 March 2026, companies generally must cancel treasury shares within one year of acquisition. Continued retention or disposal requires a statutory exception, an approved treasury-share plan and annual shareholder approval.

Key points

  • The treasury-share reform has been in force since 6 March 2026.
  • It applies to listed, unlisted and venture companies.
  • Treasury shares acquired after commencement generally must be cancelled within one year of acquisition.
  • Existing directly acquired treasury shares generally have a 6 September 2027 deadline.
  • Existing indirectly acquired shares held by a trustee are generally measured from the date they are returned to the company under the transition rule.
  • Approval of a retention/disposal plan is generally an ordinary shareholder resolution; adding a business-purpose basis to the articles requires a special resolution.
  • The plan must be approved by shareholders every year.
  • Major new-share protections, including injunctions and a six-month invalidity action, apply to treasury-share disposals.
  • Listed-company violations may expose directors personally to an administrative fine of up to KRW 50 million; unlisted companies still face director-liability and transaction-validity issues.

01. The treasury-share cancellation regime is already in force

Act No. 21448 was promulgated and entered into force on 6 March 2026.[1] A later consolidated version of the Commercial Act may show a 1 January 2027 commencement date because of other amendments, but the treasury-share cancellation duty itself is already effective. The Ministry of Justice’s 11 March 2026 guide confirms this point.[2]

02. The rules apply to all companies

Article 341-4 is a general Commercial Act rule, not merely a listed-company special provision. The Ministry identifies listed, unlisted and venture companies as within the regime.[2] The separate KRW 50 million administrative-fine provision, however, is directed at listed-company violations.

03. Acquisition and post-acquisition holding are separate questions

Article 341 generally requires shareholder approval of the type and number of shares, aggregate acquisition-price ceiling and an acquisition period of up to one year, subject to the statutory board-resolution alternative for companies whose articles permit board-approved dividends.[3] Once acquired, Article 341-4 governs whether those shares must be cancelled or may continue to be held.

04. Post-commencement treasury shares generally must be cancelled within one year

Article 341-4(1) requires a company to cancel treasury shares within one year of acquisition.[1] The rule is not limited to voluntary market purchases; treasury shares acquired through events such as mergers are also generally subject to the cancellation principle.

05. Existing directly acquired treasury shares generally face a 6 September 2027 deadline

The supplementary provisions give existing directly acquired treasury shares a transition period: they generally must be cancelled within one year after six months have elapsed from commencement. This produces a general deadline of 6 September 2027.[1]

06. Pledges, legacy bonds and trusts have separate timing rules

Where an existing directly acquired treasury share was subject to a pledge that is released after commencement, the one-year period generally runs from release. Legacy exchange/redeemable bond arrangements may have a separate one-year period from extinction of the claim or expiry of the exchange/redemption period.

For treasury shares indirectly acquired through a trust before commencement and still held by the trustee at commencement, the special transition provision generally measures the one-year period from the date the company receives the shares back from the trustee.[1] This differs from new post-commencement trust acquisitions, where the trustee’s acquisition date is treated as the listed company’s acquisition date.

07. Continued holding requires a statutory exception

Article 341-4(2) provides limited grounds for retention or disposal, including pro rata/equal disposal to shareholders, employee compensation, employee share-ownership arrangements, statutory transaction uses and necessary business purposes supported by the articles.[1] A vague intention to “use the shares later” is not itself a statutory ground.

08. Plan approval is an ordinary resolution; charter amendments require a special resolution

Approval of the treasury-share retention/disposal plan is generally governed by the ordinary resolution rule in Article 368(1), because Article 341-4 does not prescribe a special voting threshold. By contrast, adding a business-purpose ground under Article 341-4(2)(5) requires an amendment to the articles and therefore the special resolution under Article 434.[4]

09. The board prepares the plan and every director signs

The plan must state the purpose, type and number of shares, acquisition method, treasury-share position and ratio changes, intended holding period and disposal timing. Every director must sign or affix a seal. The Ministry also treats preparation of the plan as an important management act requiring a board resolution.[2]

10. Shareholder approval is required every year

Article 341-4(3) requires the retention/disposal plan to be approved by the shareholders every year.[1] Long-term holding therefore needs to be built into the annual corporate calendar.

11. Existing treasury shares may be retained if approval is obtained during the transition period

The Ministry explains that existing directly acquired shares need not have been approved at the first 2026 AGM. A company may obtain the necessary plan approval within the transition period instead of cancelling, provided a statutory exception exists.[2]

12. Cancellation itself may be approved by the board

Under amended Article 343(1), a company may cancel its treasury shares by board resolution regardless of how they were acquired.[1] The board should identify the class and number of shares and the effective date, followed by the necessary certificate, shareholder-register or electronic-registration cancellation steps.

13. Pro rata and equal disposal to shareholders is the default

Article 342(1) makes pro rata and equal access by existing shareholders the default disposal method. Third-party disposals are permitted only under the statutory grounds in Article 341-4(2)(2) through (5), such as employee compensation, employee share ownership, statutory transaction uses and qualifying business purposes.[1]

14. Share-issuance protections now substantially apply to treasury-share disposal

Article 342(4) expressly applies Articles 417–419, 421, 422, 423(2)–(3), 424, 424-2 and 427–432 to treasury-share disposals.[5] Key consequences include an injunction against unlawful or materially unfair disposal, liability for acquiring shares at a materially unfair price, and an invalidity action under Article 429. The latter generally must be brought within six months of the disposal. A statutory breach does not automatically invalidate every disposal; invalidity grounds are likely to be construed strictly in light of transaction security.[6]

15. Third-party disposal for business purposes requires caution

The “business purpose” language resembles the third-party new-share allotment rule in Article 418(2), and the Ministry expressly points to that case law as relevant.[2] Supreme Court precedent has scrutinised third-party allotments used primarily to entrench control where a genuine business need was absent.[6] Treasury-share disposals should therefore be assessed by business necessity, counterparty, pricing, scale and the impact on existing shareholder control.

16. Treasury shares carry no voting or economic shareholder rights

Article 341-3(1) expressly denies the company voting rights, pre-emptive rights, rights to receive shares through stock dividends, dividend rights and other shareholder rights in respect of treasury shares.[1]

17. Two different pledge restrictions must be distinguished

Article 341-3(3) prohibits a company from pledging treasury shares it holds. Paragraph (4) separately limits the company’s ability to take its own shares as collateral, generally to no more than one-twentieth of total issued shares, subject to statutory exceptions.[1]

18. Exchangeable or redeemable bonds backed by treasury shares are prohibited

Article 341-3(2) prohibits issuing bonds that may be exchanged for or redeemed with treasury shares.[1] Legacy instruments issued before commencement may instead fall under the supplementary transition rules.

19. Two categories of shares are excluded from merger consideration

Article 529-2 prohibits merger consideration in respect of both shares of the disappearing company already held by the surviving company and treasury shares held by the disappearing company.[1] M&A models therefore need to separate both categories when calculating consideration and post-merger ownership.

20. Treasury-share allocations and transfers are restricted in demergers

Article 530-13 prohibits allocating shares of a new or successor company in respect of the demerging company’s treasury shares and also prohibits transferring those treasury shares to the new or successor company.[1] Borderline transactions spanning the commencement date and the consequences of a breach should be analysed case by case.

21. Trust acquisitions by listed companies are subject to the same cancellation principle

For new post-commencement trust acquisitions, the trustee’s acquisition date is treated as the listed company’s acquisition date for the cancellation rule. A return of the shares after trust termination does not restart a fresh one-year period. Existing trust-held shares at commencement remain governed by the special transition rule described above.[1]

22. Special transition rules apply where cancellation affects foreign ownership caps

Where cancellation of existing treasury shares would cause foreign ownership ratios to breach statutory caps in regulated sectors such as broadcasting, telecommunications, capital markets or aviation, the amendment provides a separate transition route allowing necessary disposals within three years from commencement.[1]

23. Unlisted companies still face legal consequences even without the listed-company fine

The KRW 50 million administrative fine in Article 635(3) is directed at listed-company violations and is imposed on directors personally, not on the company itself.[8] Unlisted companies remain bound by Article 341-4. Directors may face company liability under Article 399 and, where the statutory requirements are met, third-party liability under Article 401.[7] Unlawful treasury-share disposals may also be challenged by the six-month invalidity action incorporated through Article 342(4). Missing the cancellation deadline does not automatically extinguish the shares.

24. LAW NOAH Analysis — manage treasury shares as legal positions with maturity dates

Companies should track acquisition date and method, transition status, pledges or legacy bond arrangements, statutory cancellation deadline, retention ground, charter basis, board approval, director signatures, annual shareholder approval and actual disposal against the approved plan.

For M&A due diligence, treasury-share quantity, acquisition history, cancellation deadlines, approved plans, cross-holdings in transaction counterparties, trust arrangements, exchangeable instruments and employee compensation plans should be reviewed at the structuring stage rather than left to closing housekeeping.

Conclusion

The reform’s baseline is simple: acquisition → cancellation within one year.

Continued use requires a statutory exception → any necessary charter amendment → board-approved retention/disposal plan → all-director signatures → shareholder approval → annual reapproval → holding or disposal strictly within the approved plan.

Companies that already held directly acquired treasury shares before 6 March 2026 generally face a concrete 6 September 2027 deadline. Businesses using treasury shares for M&A, employee incentives, strategic investment, control structures or financing should re-test existing arrangements under the amended Commercial Act.
