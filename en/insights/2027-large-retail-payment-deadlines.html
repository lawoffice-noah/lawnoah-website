South Korea’s 2027 Large Retail Business Act Amendments — Payment Deadlines Cut to 20 and 35 Days

From 14 October 2027, specified sales proceeds generally must be paid within 20 days after monthly sales closing and direct-purchase goods within 35 days after receipt.

Key points

  • The amendments take effect on 14 October 2027.
  • Sales proceeds for purchase-on-special-contract, consignment and specified store-lease arrangements generally must be paid within 20 days after monthly sales closing.
  • Direct-purchase goods generally must be paid within 35 days after receipt.
  • Where all direct-purchase transactions with a supplier from the first through the last day of a month are aggregated, payment may be made within 20 days after monthly purchase closing.
  • Limited exceptions are created for no-fault obstacles and, for direct purchases only, approved urgent management concerns; implementing details remain pending.
  • Late payment may trigger default interest, corrective orders, administrative surcharges and damages.

01. The amendments take effect on 14 October 2027

Act No. 22064 was promulgated on 13 October 2026 and takes effect one year later, on 14 October 2027.[1]

The one-year lead time gives retailers time to amend contracts and settlement, accounting and ERP processes.

02. Why the payment deadlines are being shortened

The reform responds to concerns that long settlement periods effectively shift working-capital burdens onto suppliers.

Following major retail-payment disruptions, the Fair Trade Commission reviewed payment practices across 11 retail sectors and 111 brands and consulted market participants before proposing the reform.[3]

04. First confirm whether the Large Retail Business Act applies

A “large retailer” generally includes a business receiving consumer goods from multiple suppliers for resale where the prior year’s retail sales are at least KRW 100 billion or the aggregate sales-floor area used for retail is at least 3,000㎡.[2]

Certain franchisors are included in the statutory definition, and Article 2-2 deems qualifying store lessors to be large retailers for specified provisions including Article 8. Transactions in which superior bargaining position is not recognised may fall outside the Act under Article 3.

05. What is a purchase-on-special-contract transaction?

In this structure, the retailer purchases goods on credit but may return unsold inventory, and after sale pays the supplier the proceeds net of an agreed sales margin.

After commencement, those proceeds generally must be paid within 20 days after monthly sales closing.

06. Consignment transactions also move to 20 days

In a consignment structure, the retailer sells the supplier’s goods in its own name and remits the sales proceeds after deducting an agreed commission.

The statutory deadline likewise becomes 20 days after monthly sales closing.

07. Certain store-lease sales proceeds also move to 20 days

Article 8(1) also covers qualifying arrangements where the large retailer receives and manages the store tenant’s sales proceeds before settlement.

Those arrangements therefore require review under the new 20-day deadline.

08. Direct purchases generally move to 35 days after receipt

In a direct purchase, the retailer purchases the goods and bears the risk of unsold inventory.

The current 60-day deadline is shortened to 35 days after the retailer receives the goods.[1]

09. Direct purchases are not always measured from receipt plus 35 days

The amended Article 8(2) itself creates a separate monthly-settlement rule.

Where, for each supplier, all goods transactions from the first through the last day of the month are aggregated for payment, payment may be made within 20 days after monthly purchase closing.[1]

10. Why the monthly direct-purchase settlement rule was created

Monthly settlement is common in direct purchasing. A pure 35-day receipt rule could make an ordinary month-end settlement cycle unlawful for goods received early in the month.

The separate 20-day rule preserves a monthly settlement structure while materially shortening the overall payment cycle.[3]

11. The statutory conditions for monthly settlement matter

The special rule does not apply merely because the retailer pays once a month.

The statute refers to aggregation by supplier, for all goods transactions from the first through the last day of the month. Retailers should verify that their ERP and purchasing systems actually satisfy those conditions.

12. The 20-day rule for special-contract and similar sales runs from monthly sales closing

For Article 8(1) transactions, the trigger is the monthly sales closing date, not the delivery date.

Retailers should distinguish the underlying sale, monthly sales closing and actual payment date in their systems.

13. The 35-day direct-purchase rule runs from receipt of the goods

For ordinary direct purchases, the statutory clock starts when the retailer receives the goods, not when the retailer later sells them to consumers.

Slow sales or remaining inventory do not automatically extend the statutory deadline.

14. Around commencement, focus on the actual transaction rather than only the contract date

The supplementary provisions apply the amended Article 8(1) to cases falling within the relevant Article 8(1) categories on or after 14 October 2027.[1]

The FTC explains the rule as applying to relevant post-commencement sales and similar conduct. The date of the master agreement is therefore not the only relevant date.

For direct purchases, the amendment applies where goods are received through a direct-purchase transaction after commencement.

15. A new exception addresses attachment and other no-fault obstacles

The amendment creates an exception where payment cannot be made because of a natural disaster, attachment of the supplier’s claim or another circumstance for which the retailer is not responsible, if the requirements to be prescribed by Presidential Decree are met.[1]

Under the previous law, Supreme Court case 2016Du55896 held that service of a provisional attachment alone did not eliminate delay liability; the third-party debtor could use a court deposit to avoid double-payment risk and delay liability.[5]

The amendment creates a new statutory route for no-fault obstacles. The FTC has discussed court-deposit mechanisms, but the detailed requirements have not yet been finalised in the implementing decree.

16. The urgent-management exception applies only to direct purchases

For direct purchases only, the amended statute expressly addresses bankruptcy, rehabilitation, a rapid deterioration in cash flow and other urgent management concerns.[1]

The exception is not automatic: the retailer must obtain FTC approval under procedures to be specified by Presidential Decree. It does not operate as a general exception for Article 8(1) special-contract or consignment transactions.

17. Ordinary cash-flow pressure does not automatically extend the deadline

The provision is not a general permission to delay suppliers whenever liquidity becomes tight.

Both urgent management circumstances and FTC approval are required. Detailed financial criteria and application procedures remain to be defined by implementing regulations.

18. Default interest applies after the statutory deadline

Article 8(3) requires default interest for the period beyond the statutory deadline.

The statutory ceiling is 40% per annum. As of 12 November 2026, FTC Notice No. 2021-13 sets the applicable rate at 15.5% per annum.[4]

Because the amended deadlines take effect in October 2027, businesses should confirm the notice rate in force when any actual delay occurs.

19. What if the contract states a later payment date?

A later contractual date does not automatically displace Article 8.

In Supreme Court case 2016Du55896, the Court held that departure from the statutory payment deadline and default-interest regime requires proof that the supplier voluntarily agreed to the different arrangement, and the burden of proving that voluntary consent rests on the large retailer asserting exclusion of Article 8.[5]

A supplier’s signature on retailer-drafted standard terms alone may therefore be insufficient. Existing master agreements and standard terms should be revised before commencement.

20. Payment cannot be substituted with gift certificates or goods

Article 8(4) prohibits payment of sales proceeds, goods payments and default interest with gift certificates or goods.[2]

21. Late payment may trigger corrective orders and administrative surcharges

Article 8 violations may be addressed through FTC corrective orders under Article 32, including orders to pay the relevant sales proceeds or goods payments.

Article 35(1) expressly covers violations of Articles 7 through 10, so Article 8 violations are also within the administrative-surcharge regime.[2]

22. Damages liability may also arise

Article 35-2(1) generally requires a large retailer that causes loss by violating the Act to compensate the supplier, subject to the statutory no-fault defence.

Supreme Court case 2016Du55896 also recognised that delay based on disadvantageous payment terms without voluntary supplier consent may constitute a wrongful act supporting damages equivalent to delayed-payment interest.[5]

Article 8 itself is not among the provisions listed for treble damages in Article 35-2(2), so a payment-deadline violation is not automatically a treble-damages claim.

23. Late payment itself is not automatically a criminal offence

Article 39 does not separately list an Article 8 payment-deadline violation as a direct criminal offence.

But a two-step route may arise: Article 8 violation → FTC corrective order under Article 32 → failure to comply with that order. Failure to comply with the corrective order may then trigger criminal liability under Article 39(1)(5).

24. The amendment changes the retailer’s payment calendar

Updating the contract wording is not enough. Retailers should review receipt dates, sales dates, monthly sales and purchase closings, supplier-level aggregation, invoices, returns and offsets, internal approvals, scheduled payment dates, default-interest calculations and exception workflows.

The shorter 20- and 35-day cycles may require changes to ERP, accounting and treasury workflows.

25. What suppliers should monitor

Suppliers should independently track transaction type, receipt date, sales and purchase closing dates, whether the monthly direct-purchase rule actually applies, statutory due dates, default interest and any asserted reason for withholding payment.

The actual transaction and settlement structure matters more than the label placed on the contract.

27. Checklist for suppliers

  • Retain evidence of actual goods-receipt dates.
  • Confirm monthly sales and purchase closing dates.
  • Check whether the contractual label matches actual operations.
  • Calculate the statutory due date independently.
  • Identify overdue unpaid amounts.
  • Check whether default interest was paid.
  • Review unilateral withholding or settlement deferrals.
  • Where attachment is cited, check the implementing requirements in force at that time.
  • Monitor whether offsets are being used as a de facto payment delay.
  • Preserve records of repeated payment delays.

28. LAW NOAH Analysis — the real issue is cash flow, not just the settlement date

On paper, the reform looks simple: 40 days become 20, and 60 days become 35.

Operationally, the effect is much larger. Large retailers must fund suppliers sooner and redesign the interaction among working capital, inventory turnover, invoicing, settlement and internal approval. In direct purchasing, the retailer may owe payment within 35 days even before the goods are sold to consumers, shifting inventory and financing risk more clearly toward the retailer.

The statute nevertheless preserves practical monthly settlement through a specific monthly rule and creates limited exceptions for no-fault obstacles and approved urgent management concerns.

The correct response is therefore not merely to replace “60 days” with “35 days” in a template. Retailers need systems that know when goods were received, when sales closed, which transactions were aggregated, when payment is due, and what legal process applies if payment genuinely cannot be made.
