South Korea’s 2026 Nationality Act Amendment — Simplified Naturalisation and Multiple Nationality for Spouses of Independence Patriots’ Lineal Relatives

The Nationality Act amendment scheduled to take effect on 3 December 2026 creates a new simplified-naturalisation route for a foreign national married to a Korean national who is a lineal ascendant or descendant of a recognised independence patriot. It relaxes the Korean-domicile period and updates the related nationality-deliberation and foreign-nationality non-exercise pledge provisions.[1]

Eligibility requires separate consideration of the qualifying family relationship, marriage and domicile periods, remaining naturalisation requirements and post-acquisition procedures. This report compares the new route with existing rules and examines the forthcoming change to related veterans legislation.

Key takeaways

  • This is not a relaxation for every foreign spouse. The new route’s family, marriage and domicile conditions must be checked together.[1]
  • The exceptions concern Article 5, items 1 and 1-2. Adulthood, conduct, livelihood, basic knowledge and the national-security requirement in item 6 remain separate matters for examination.[1][2]
  • Veterans-law family categories must be checked at the relevant time. A separate amendment will include certain great-grandchildren from 2027.[7]
  • The non-exercise pledge has a deadline and filing requirements. Permission for naturalisation does not itself resolve retention of foreign nationality or every subsequent step.[8]

01. Distinguish the December Nationality Amendment from Next Year’s Veterans-Law Change

The Nationality Act amendment was promulgated as Act No. 21725 on 2 June 2026 and takes effect on 3 December 2026. It adds Article 6(3), expands eligibility for the pledge not to exercise foreign nationality under Article 10(2)1, and updates the matters referred to the Nationality Deliberation Committee under Article 22.[1]

The following dates should be considered together.

LegislationCommencementMain change
Nationality Act Amendment No. 217253 December 2026A new simplified-naturalisation route and related pledge and deliberation provisions
Family-eligibility provisions in Amendment No. 21616 to the Act on the Honorable Treatment of Persons of Distinguished Service to Independence1 January 2027Inclusion of certain children of grandchildren within the eligible bereaved-family and family categories, subject to conditions

These are amendments to different statutes. However, the new naturalisation provision refers to the family categories in the independence-patriot legislation. Checking the Nationality Act without checking when the related legislation changes can therefore leave an important gap.[1][7]

Separate forthcoming provisions from routes already available

This report explains a special route that has not yet taken effect. Applicants should first examine existing naturalisation routes and distinguish them from preparations dependent on commencement of the new provisions.

02. The Route Is for Foreign Spouses of Korean Nationals with the Specified Family Relationship

New Article 6(3) uses the wording “lineal ascendants or descendants of a recognised independence patriot” (독립유공자의 직계 존속ㆍ비속). The following requirements must be checked together.[1]

ItemRequirement under the new route
ApplicantA foreign national
Spouse’s nationalityA national of the Republic of Korea
Spouse’s family relationshipA lineal ascendant or descendant of a person recognised under Article 4 of the Act on the Honorable Treatment of Persons of Distinguished Service to Independence
Marriage durationTen years have elapsed since marriage to that spouse
Domicile in KoreaContinuous domicile in the Republic of Korea for at least three months while married

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Being descended from an independence patriot does not, by itself, establish that the spouse is a Korean national. Marriage duration and the period of domicile in Korea are also separate requirements.

A useful practical approach is to separate the following connections.

Recognised independence-patriot status → lineal relationship to the Korean spouse → the spouse’s current nationality → marriage to the applicant

Identifying the records supporting each connection helps distinguish what the family can explain from what requires additional official documentation.

Confirm recognised status through official records

Article 4 of the independence-patriot legislation covers the categories of patriots who died for independence and patriots who contributed to independence. It requires the specified independence-related service and the associated award of an Order of Merit for National Foundation, National Foundation Medal or Presidential Commendation, as applicable. A family account of participation in the independence movement must be distinguished from recognition within the statutory category.[6]

Preparation can include checking award and registration records with the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs or the competent veterans office, then connecting them to family-status records. The Ministry’s registration guidance likewise separates evidence of awards from evidence of family relationships.[6]

However, veterans registration and eligibility for the new naturalisation route are separate systems. Documents identified in veterans-registration guidance should not simply be presented as the final mandatory document list for this naturalisation route.

03. Compare Existing Marriage-Based Routes with the New Route

Existing Article 6(2) provides for simplified naturalisation of foreign spouses of Korean nationals. The amendment adds paragraph (3); it does not abolish the existing routes.[1][3]

RoutePrincipal marriage and domicile requirements
Article 6(2)1Continuous domicile in Korea for at least two years while married to the Korean spouse
Article 6(2)2Three years have elapsed since marriage, with domicile in Korea for at least one year while married
New Article 6(3)Ten years have elapsed since marriage to the qualifying spouse, with domicile in Korea for at least three months while married

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

This table compares the principal time requirements, not exemptions from every other naturalisation condition. A person already meeting an existing route should first examine whether there is any need to wait for the new one.

Existing exceptions concerning death, disappearance and care of a minor child

Article 6(2)3 and 4 provide separate routes with the following principal requirements.[3]

ProvisionPrincipal requirements
Item 3Before completing the period under item 1 or 2, the applicant was domiciled in Korea while married to a Korean national and became unable to maintain a normal marital life because of the spouse’s death or disappearance, or another reason not attributable to the applicant. The remaining period must be completed, and the Minister of Justice must consider the case appropriate.
Item 4Although the applicant has not met the requirements under item 1 or 2, the applicant is raising, or is required to raise, a minor child born of that marriage. The period under item 1 or 2 must be completed, and the Minister of Justice must consider the case appropriate.

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

A spouse’s death or the existence of a child does not remove all time and assessment requirements. Nor should these provisions automatically be imported as exceptions to new paragraph (3).

Where the marital relationship has changed, examine separately whether the new route is available and whether another route under existing paragraph (2) applies.

04. Relaxed Requirements and Continuing Examination Are Different Matters

The new route creates exceptions to the ordinary requirements of at least five years’ domicile in Korea and a status of stay permitting permanent residence. It does not waive all the remaining requirements in Article 5.[1][2]

Requirement in Article 5 of the Nationality ActPosition under the new route
Item 1: continuous domicile in Korea for at least five yearsException applies; assess the new route’s own domicile requirement instead
Item 1-2: a status of stay permitting permanent residenceException applies
Item 2: adulthood under the Korean Civil ActExamined separately
Item 3: good conduct, including compliance with lawsExamined separately
Item 4: ability to support oneself through assets or skills, or through family members sharing a livelihoodExamined separately
Item 5: basic knowledge, including Korean-language ability and understanding of Korean customsExamined separately
Item 6: the Minister of Justice’s recognition that permission for naturalisation would not harm national security, maintenance of order or public welfareExamined separately

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

National security and the related considerations are not merely factors mentioned when discussing discretion under Article 4. They are expressly stated as a requirement in current Article 5, item 6 and should not be omitted from the ordinary naturalisation requirements.

The Supreme Court has distinguished satisfaction of naturalisation requirements from the Minister’s discretion over permission. That general principle is not a basis for treating the express requirement now in Article 5, item 6 as absent.[11]

Special naturalisation also distinguishes descendants from their spouses

Article 6(1)1(a) of the Enforcement Decree identifies, as one category for special naturalisation, a person who is themselves a recognised independence patriot, or whose spouse or lineal ascendant or descendant is such a person.[4]

The family relationship must be assessed from the applicant’s position. A grandchild of an independence patriot and a foreign national married to that grandchild have different relationships to the patriot. One spouse’s eligibility for special naturalisation does not automatically pass the same eligibility on to the other spouse.

Whether the applicant independently has another ground for special naturalisation can be examined separately. The new simplified-naturalisation route and special naturalisation should not be confused; the applicant’s own grounds must be identified.[4]

05. Check the Veterans-Law Amendment When Assessing Committee Deliberation

The proviso to new Article 6(3) requires deliberation by the Nationality Deliberation Committee where the Korean spouse falls outside the bereaved-family and family categories in Article 5 of the independence-patriot legislation. It does not ask whether the foreign applicant is within those categories.[1]

The current statutory categories include spouses, children, grandchildren and daughters-in-law meeting specified conditions, with separate conditions for each category. They should not be equated with all lineal descendants.[6]

An example involving great-grandchildren must identify the applicable date

A great-grandchild is a lineal descendant, but that relationship alone does not automatically place the person within the current Article 5 family categories. Stopping the explanation there, however, could mislead readers about future application.[6][7]

Separate Amendment No. 21616 adds Article 5(1)3-2 and paragraph (5) from 1 January 2027, including certain children of grandchildren subject to conditions. The principal conditions include the following.[7]

IssuePrincipal condition in the amendment
Earlier generations’ compensation historyThe bereaved family members under items 1 and 2 died without receiving compensation
Compensation to a grandchildThere is no bereaved family member under item 3 receiving compensation
More than one qualifying personLimited to one person with priority under the statutory payment order
Adopted childrenSeparate conditions apply concerning the circumstances and timing of adoption

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Neither “all great-grandchildren qualify” nor “great-grandchildren are always excluded” is an appropriate general statement. An individual case requires examination of Article 5 as applicable at the relevant time, each condition and the transitional provisions.

Committee deliberation is not the same step as permission for naturalisation

Even where committee deliberation is required, the other requirements, including family relationships and time periods, must be examined separately. Being subject to deliberation does not guarantee permission.

The proviso to Article 22(2) also contemplates exceptions to deliberation prescribed by Presidential Decree. Existing decree exceptions concerning special naturalisation or renunciation of Korean nationality should not simply be assumed to apply unchanged to the new simplified route. Check the subordinate rules and guidance applicable upon commencement.[1][10]

Veterans-registration records and actual receipt of compensation should also be distinguished. Whether a person is currently receiving money should not be used as the sole test for the whole statutory family category.

06. Review Domicile Periods Together with the Actual Marital Relationship

Article 5 of the Enforcement Rule calculates the relevant domicile period by reference to lawful, continuous stay in Korea after lawful entry and completion of foreigner registration. This is different from adding together days spent on visits.[5]

Departure does not necessarily restart the calculation in every case. The same provision addresses, among other cases, re-entry within the validity of a re-entry permit, temporary departure where an extension of stay was unavailable followed by qualifying re-entry, and comparable circumstances in which the Minister considers aggregation appropriate.[5]

A practical chronology should connect entry, registration, authorised periods of stay, domicile, and the grounds and dates of departure and re-entry. Relaxation of the permanent-residence-status requirement does not remove the need for lawful stay.

Read earlier case law alongside the current rules

In Supreme Court judgment 2009Du19069, delivered on 15 July 2010, the Court held, in relation to the domestic-residence requirement for simplified naturalisation under the law then applicable, that the calculation should not differ solely according to the category of stay.[11]

This does not mean that periods of unlawful stay are always included, or that every type of stay under the forthcoming special route has already been definitively accepted. The current Enforcement Rule’s lawful-stay requirement and the facts of the particular case must be considered together.[5]

Do not assume that examination ends with the marriage registration

The new provision requires a marriage period and continuing married status. It is therefore useful to prepare material explaining the actual relationship, not only the marriage date recorded in a document.

Current Article 4 of the Enforcement Decree provides for measures including requests to attend or submit documents and on-site checks at the place of residence to examine naturalisation requirements. The wording on a normal marital relationship in the former Decree should not be cited as the content of current Article 4(2).[10]

Without presenting detailed examination standards or case law for new paragraph (3) as already settled, a sensible approach is to identify circumstances that could raise questions about the genuineness of the marriage and prepare an explanation supported by records. Where the spouses have lived apart because of overseas work, for example, the reasons and the actual relationship can be explained specifically.

07. The Non-Exercise Pledge Has Eligibility, Timing and Korean-Domicile Requirements

The amendment adds new Article 6(3) to Article 10(2)1. Naturalised persons falling within that category are therefore included among those who may make a pledge not to exercise foreign nationality instead of renouncing it.[1]

Article 10 generally requires renunciation of foreign nationality within one year after acquiring Korean nationality. Persons within the statutory exceptions must renounce or make the pledge within the same period. Failure to comply results in loss of Korean nationality when that period expires.[8]

IssuePoint to check
Eligibility to pledge under the new routeWhether the relevant ground exists when permission for naturalisation is granted
Deadline to renounce or pledgeOne year from actual acquisition of Korean nationality
Position when filing the pledgeIn principle, the person must be domiciled in Korea
Follow-up after submissionAcceptance of the pledge, issue of the confirmation document and related processing

Scroll horizontally to read the full table.

Article 11(3) of the Enforcement Decree requires submission of the pledge while domiciled in Korea. Provisions permitting filing through overseas diplomatic missions in certain cases, such as reinstatement of nationality or acquisition by acknowledgement of parentage, do not automatically extend to this naturalisation route.[8]

A person planning to move their living arrangements abroad immediately after naturalisation should therefore consider not only eligibility to pledge, but also the filing date and maintenance of domicile in Korea.

Check the position of a multiple national under Korean law

Article 11-2(1) of the Nationality Act and Article 16(1) of the Enforcement Decree govern multiple-nationality status. A person who makes the non-exercise pledge and holds both Korean and foreign nationality is treated solely as a Korean national when Korean laws apply.[9]

This does not mean the person may freely choose to be treated as a foreign national within Korea according to the circumstances. Conversely, Korean permission to make the pledge does not determine the other country’s nationality law. Anyone intending to retain their existing nationality should examine the effect of acquiring Korean nationality under that country’s law as well.

08. Three Months Is Not a Processing Deadline, and Further Checks Follow Permission

The new route’s domicile period should not be confused with a deadline for completing the naturalisation examination. The process involves application, examination, permission and acquisition of nationality. In principle, the oath of allegiance and conferment of the naturalisation certificate are connected to the acquisition stage.[13]

For foreign-language documents, check the translation and the translator’s name and contact details required by Article 16 of the Enforcement Rule. The receiving office’s guidance should be consulted on authentication of foreign-issued documents and the precise supporting-document requirements.[13]

Permission obtained by false or improper means can be revoked

Article 21 of the Nationality Act permits revocation of permission obtained by false or other improper means. Article 27 of the Enforcement Decree specifies circumstances including convictions concerning forged or altered status documents, and final determinations invalidating or rescinding a legal relationship on which acquisition of nationality was based.[12]

Accurate family, marriage and immigration records therefore matter both during the application and after permission is granted. However, divorce after naturalisation should not be described as automatically revoking every naturalisation permission. The circumstances at acquisition and the legal ground for revocation must be examined separately.

Revocation generally requires an opportunity for the person concerned to explain their position, subject to statutory exceptions. This is distinct from loss of nationality under Article 10 for failure to renounce foreign nationality or make the pledge.[8][12]

Distinguish preparation before commencement from changing an application route

Family and immigration histories can be organised before commencement. It should not, however, be stated categorically that an existing application automatically receives the new treatment, or that every applicant must withdraw and reapply.

The route should be chosen after checking the actual filing and examination stage, the applicable legislation and the guidance issued for commencement. Starting document preparation and determining the applicable legal regime are different steps.

09. LAW NOAH Analysis — Separate Family Relationships, Timelines and Post-Acquisition Plans

The following are practical suggestions for assessing eligibility and preparation, not a separate statutory form.

1) Assess each applicant rather than the family as a whole

Within one family, a descendant of an independence patriot, their spouse and their children may have different procedures available.

A family tree that separately records current nationality, the history of acquiring Korean nationality, the relationship to the independence patriot and the marriage can be useful. The starting point is not to assume that one person’s special-naturalisation eligibility or acquisition of nationality automatically determines another family member’s position.

2) Put marriage and domicile periods alongside legislative commencement dates

Calculate the marriage and domicile periods separately, but also enter commencement of the Nationality Act and the related veterans legislation on the same chronology.

Marriage → spouse’s nationality acquisition or change → entry and registration → departure and re-entry → application and examination → commencement of the relevant legislation

This makes it easier to examine whether an existing route is already satisfied, whether there is a reason to wait for the new route and when the need for committee deliberation should be reconsidered.

3) Plan beyond permission for naturalisation

Preparation does not end with assessing the prospect of permission. The actual acquisition date, treatment of foreign nationality, pledge deadline and Korean-domicile requirement also need to be tracked.

Where overseas relocation or business travel is planned, check whether it conflicts with pledge filing. Obtaining permission and complying with post-acquisition obligations are connected but separate steps.
