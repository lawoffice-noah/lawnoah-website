South Korea’s 2027 Unfair Competition Prevention Act Amendments — Trade-Secret Brokerage, Hacking and Post-Acquisition Use or Disclosure

From 16 March 2027, brokerage or inducement of trade-secret infringement becomes an express infringement category, hacking is expressly listed as an improper acquisition method, and criminal rules for subsequent use or disclosure are strengthened.

Key points

  • The amendments take effect on 16 March 2027.
  • Introducing, brokering or inducing specified trade-secret infringement becomes an express infringement category.
  • Hacking is expressly added to the statutory list of improper acquisition methods.
  • Criminal liability is strengthened for subsequent use and disclosure to a third party after improper acquisition.
  • Ordinary recruitment from a competitor is not prohibited as such, but recruitment tied to delivery of trade secrets creates substantial risk.
  • Companies should review recruitment, headhunting, onboarding/offboarding and information-security controls together.

01. The trade-secret regime changes on 16 March 2027

The amended Unfair Competition Prevention and Trade Secret Protection Act was promulgated as Act No. 21952 on 15 September 2026 and takes effect six months later, on 16 March 2027.[1]

Existing law already regulated improper acquisition, use and disclosure of trade secrets. The amendments more expressly address intermediaries, hacking, post-acquisition use or disclosure, and further disclosure by downstream recipients.

02. Why the Act was amended

Technology leakage does not necessarily occur through a single employee copying files before departure. In practice, a sequence may involve identifying key personnel → making contact → offering a move → inducing information delivery → acquiring trade secrets → using them → passing them onward.

The Korean Intellectual Property Office explained that brokers posing as recruitment intermediaries had become involved in transfers of key personnel and leakage of trade secrets, while direct legal grounds for sanctioning the brokerage conduct itself were limited.[2]

03. Why technology-leak brokers were difficult to regulate directly

According to the agency’s explanatory material, ordinary criminal-law theories of instigation or aiding did not always fit brokerage of trade-secret leakage. In some cases, sanctions instead rested on unregistered or unlicensed employment-intermediation offences under the Employment Security Act. The agency itself cites Suwon District Court cases 2021godan2363 and 2021no7822 as examples.[2]

The amendment is designed to close that regulatory gap by adding introduction, brokerage and inducement as an independent form of trade-secret infringement.

The amendment to Article 2(3)(a) is limited to expressly adding hacking as an improper means. The criminal-law expansion for post-acquisition use or disclosure is instead an amendment to Article 18(1)(2).[1]

05. Brokerage, introduction and inducement become express infringement acts

New Article 2(3)(g) adds the introduction, brokerage or inducement of any conduct falling within Article 2(3)(a) through (f) as a trade-secret infringement act.[1]

The rule therefore reaches not only the person who directly acquires or uses the secret, but also a person who connects participants or actively induces the infringement process.

06. Must the underlying leak actually occur?

The new provision treats introduction, brokerage and inducement as a separate infringement category. The agency also describes the amendment as allowing regulation from the preparatory stage through acquisition, use and disclosure.[2]

Because the provision has not yet taken effect, however, there is no developed case law defining precisely when such conduct is complete or how far the underlying infringement must have progressed. It would therefore be unsafe to say either that liability always requires an actual leak or that mere contact automatically constitutes infringement.

07. Is a separate improper-purpose requirement necessary?

New Article 2(3)(g) does not itself contain an express requirement that the intermediary act for an improper gain or for the purpose of causing loss to the trade-secret holder.

But the provision cross-references the underlying infringement categories in Article 2(3)(a) through (f), which have different elements. Some underlying categories contain purpose requirements while others do not. The precise subjective element for the new provision will need to be developed through post-commencement interpretation and case law.

08. Technology-leak brokers are a principal target

The government identifies the so-called technology-leak broker as a principal target. High-risk conduct may include identifying an employee with access to core technology, negotiating a move on the condition that trade secrets be delivered, connecting that employee to a competitor, or inducing delivery of specific technical materials.[2]

The statutory wording is not limited to a particular occupation. Depending on what they actually do, internal employees, external advisers or business partners may also fall within the provision.

09. Hiring a competitor’s employee is not prohibited as such

Hiring an employee from a competitor does not, by itself, establish trade-secret infringement.

In Supreme Court case 2009Da12528, the former employee had copied the prior employer’s trade-secret documents and stored them on a laptop supplied by the new employer, but the Court held that this fact alone did not establish that the new employer’s representatives had conspired in the infringement or acquired or used the secrets.[4]

The real question is therefore not whether the company hired a competitor’s employee, but how the company participated in any infringement of the former employer’s trade secrets.

10. The analysis changes where hiring is tied to trade-secret delivery

The Supreme Court has long interpreted “improper means” broadly. In case 96Da16605, it explained that the concept may include conduct contrary to fair competition, including inducing a breach of a confidentiality obligation, in addition to theft, deception or threats.[3]

Even before the 2027 amendment, active inducement of trade-secret leakage could therefore be regulated through the concept of improper acquisition. The amendment’s significance is that introduction, brokerage and inducement are now expressly enumerated as independent infringement conduct.

12. Hacking is expressly listed as an improper acquisition method

The amendment expressly adds hacking to the list of improper acquisition methods in Article 2(3)(a).[1]

Hacking could already fall within “other improper means,” but the amendment makes clear on the face of the statute that cyber intrusion used to obtain trade secrets is covered. The agency describes the change in the same terms.[2]

13. Trade-secret protection and cybersecurity now converge

Trade secrets are stored not only in paper documents but across servers, cloud storage, email, source-code repositories, ERP/CRM systems and collaboration tools.

Companies therefore need controls capable of identifying account compromise, phishing, unauthorised access, bulk downloads, transfers to personal email or cloud accounts, and removable-media extraction. The amendment reinforces the practical convergence of trade-secret compliance and cybersecurity.

14. Why the post-acquisition use/disclosure amendment matters

Civil infringement rules must be distinguished from criminal provisions. Article 2(3)(a) already treats not only improper acquisition but also the use or disclosure of the improperly acquired trade secret as infringement.

The criminal structure was narrower. Former Article 18(1)(2) addressed the act of acquiring a trade secret by theft, deception, threats or other improper means. Subsequent use or disclosure therefore had to be addressed through Article 18(1)(1), which carried an additional improper-purpose element.

The amendment now adds to Article 18(1)(2) the use of the acquired trade secret or its disclosure to a third party.[1] Once improper acquisition is established, the amendment thus supplies a criminal basis for later use or disclosure without requiring the same additional improper-purpose element that applied under paragraph (1). The agency expressly identifies this as a key effect of the amendment.[2]

15. Further disclosure by a downstream recipient is expressly covered

Article 18(1)(3) is also amended. It previously focused on acquiring or using a trade secret while knowing that conduct under paragraphs (1) or (2) had intervened. The amendment adds disclosure to a third party.[1]

This makes the criminal treatment of onward disclosure by a downstream recipient more explicit.

16. Civil injunction and prevention claims remain available

Because the new introduction, brokerage and inducement conduct is placed within Article 2(3)’s definition of trade-secret infringement, a trade-secret holder may seek an injunction or preventive relief where its business interests are infringed or threatened.[1]

Depending on the circumstances, additional measures such as destruction of items created by the infringement or removal of facilities used for the infringement may also be sought.

17. Damages may also follow

Article 11 imposes damages liability where intentional or negligent trade-secret infringement causes loss to the holder’s business interests.

Because the new brokerage provision is an infringement act under Article 2(3), damages may arise where the statutory requirements are satisfied. The supplementary provisions specify, however, that damages under Article 11 for violations of the new provision apply to violations occurring on or after 16 March 2027.[1]

18. Intentional infringement may trigger up to fivefold damages

Article 14-2(6) allows a court, for intentional trade-secret infringement, to set damages at up to five times the amount otherwise recognised as loss.

Because the new brokerage category is itself a trade-secret infringement act, the fivefold-damages framework may in principle apply where damages liability is established and the conduct is intentional. Fivefold damages are not automatic. The court considers factors including superior bargaining position, degree of intent or awareness of likely harm, scale of damage, economic benefit gained, duration and frequency of infringement, fines, financial condition and remedial efforts.

19. How criminal liability expands

New Article 18(1)(4) criminalises the introduction, brokerage or inducement of conduct falling within Article 18(1)(1) through (3).[1]

Paragraph (4) itself does not add a separate improper-gain or harm-purpose phrase. But the conduct being brokered may fall under provisions with different subjective elements. The exact mental-state analysis for the new offence will require future case law.

Where the trade secret is used abroad, or the actor knows it will be used abroad, conduct under Article 18(1) is subject to up to 15 years’ imprisonment or a KRW 1.5 billion fine. Conduct falling within the same Article 18(1) paragraphs without that foreign-use condition is punishable under Article 18(2) by up to 10 years’ imprisonment or a KRW 500 million fine. Separate gain-linked fine rules may increase the monetary exposure.

20. Companies may also face liability — but attempts must be distinguished

Under Article 19, where a representative, agent, employee or other worker commits a completed offence under Article 18(1) through (5) in connection with the entity’s business, the entity may also be subject to a fine. The corporate fine may reach three times the fine prescribed in the relevant provision, although Article 18’s gain-linked structure means a single fixed monetary figure should not be described as an absolute ceiling.

The statutory due-care defence makes real compliance important: recruitment rules, trade-secret training, access controls, monitoring, reporting, investigations and disciplinary procedures should operate in practice rather than exist only on paper.

Article 18-2 separately punishes attempts of offences under Article 18(1) and (2). The Supreme Court has held, however, that Article 19 does not extend its dual-liability rule to Article 18-2 attempts because the statutory text does not include Article 18-2.[6] Individual attempt liability and corporate dual liability must therefore be analysed separately.

21. How acts around the commencement date should be analysed

Criminal law requires careful attention to when the relevant act occurred. Conduct completed before 16 March 2027 cannot simply be punished retroactively under a newly created offence.

If a person acquired a trade secret before commencement but then carries out a new act of use or disclosure after commencement, the post-commencement conduct must be analysed separately. In Supreme Court case 2008Do9433, concerning an earlier statutory amendment, the Court held that a later improper use could be governed by the amended law even though the trade secret had been acquired before commencement.[5] Because the supplementary provisions in that earlier amendment were not identical to the 2027 amendment, the decision should be treated as guidance on identifying a distinct post-commencement act rather than mechanically transplanted.

Supreme Court case 2025Do21522 likewise emphasised the non-retroactivity principle in connection with a previously created offence of continued possession after a return/deletion demand: conduct already complete before commencement could not be punished under the new provision merely because the state of possession continued afterward.[7]

22. Not every important company document is a trade secret

The broader rules still begin with the threshold question whether the information is legally a trade secret. The Act requires information that is not publicly known, has independent economic value and is managed as confidential.

In Supreme Court case 2009Da12528, some company documents were found to have economic utility and confidentiality-management characteristics sufficient to qualify as trade secrets.[4] A company cannot therefore rely merely on saying after the fact that information was important. Access controls, confidentiality markings, security policies, training and export restrictions are practical evidence of actual confidentiality management.

23. LAW NOAH Analysis — managing only the person who takes the file is no longer enough

The amendment matters not simply because it adds several offences. More fundamentally, it treats trade-secret leakage as a process rather than a single file-removal event.

Companies need to manage risk in two directions: preventing their own trade secrets from leaving, and preventing another company’s trade secrets from entering.

When recruiting from a competitor, the company is hiring the person’s experience and expertise, not the confidential materials the person encountered at the former employer. Even if the company never directly says “bring the files,” using an intermediary to locate personnel able to provide a competitor’s trade secrets or negotiating employment terms around delivery of specific material may itself become an independent legal issue under the new regime.

The practical response is not to suppress lateral hiring. It is to build internal controls that separate the movement of talent from the movement of trade secrets.
